# The book: D1 audit and outline

*Game Design Technology: The Holy Grail of Fun*, by Reactorcore. This is the D1 deliverable from `workflow/site-plan.md`: an audit of the sources, a proposed table of contents, the session split and a voice guide. **Status: approved by Reactorcore, with the decisions recorded under "Decisions" at the end. D2 starts from here.**

Reactorcore on the original: it was written by pouring everything out as it came, with no structure or revision. The ideas, messages, concepts and warnings are what matter. The format and organization get a full overhaul, and that is left to these sessions.

Sources read in full for this audit: `reference/bible/game-design-technology-original.html` (61,161 words by plain-text count), `2a-bible.md` and `2b-bible.md`, and Reactorcore's articles in `site/articles/`: Games Are Need Satisfiers, Where Does Meaning Come From, Throwaway Games, Future of Games, Infinite Metagame Pacing, Player-Run Services, Additive vs Multiplicative, What Is a Game Designer, Getting Started, How to Grow, Evolution of a Game Designer, Scope Creep, Forking, Competitive Games, Universe Life and Death 1 and 2. From Reactorcore's Big Projects I read only the General Design Tenets, and from the Astroneer 8th Planet proposal only the metagame parts, because the rest is project showcase. The two guest articles were skipped.

## 1. What the original covers well

The original is really two finished blocks and a skeleton. By word count, about 45,000 of the 61,000 words are Part One ("the common mistake", the extrinsic-goals warning and the intention-vs-action scenarios), about 7,000 are the Mindset, and the Technical Side is a list of headings. The finished parts are strong.

- **The definitions.** Fun is the fulfillment of human needs. Design includes marketing, publishing, maintenance and updates. A game has no intent of real-life consequence, an interactive tool does, and gamification is a tool disguised as a game. "Content" is anything consumable, in the widest sense. The rest of the book stands on these.
- **The common mistake: final result vs root cause.** It is stated clearly, then shown three times through "Unaware Beginner vs Experienced Specialist": a new game (the giant robot anime, the energetic music and the bike rides that inspired it), a sequel to an old game, and joining someone else's project. The giant robot example is the best passage in the book: personal, concrete, and it ends in a real decision (3D third-person beats 2D side view because freedom and speed need a far horizon). The four-step option process (lay out the options, get familiar with each, compare each to the purpose, choose) and "I don't know, but I'll find out" are practical tools.
- **The partner interview kit.** Six core questions (what, why, inspired by, final result, success, failure), each with a feelings-and-needs follow-up, plus "Why do you think this will work?" and "How would you achieve that?". It's ready to use as it stands.
- **Question Everything.** The list of traps (popularity, profitability, trends, tradition, originality, someone said so, forecasting, mistaking one failure for all), cognitive biases boiled down to "believing without verifying", and "why" as x-ray vision.
- **The Holy Grail.** Understand the technology, understand human beings, have a clear purpose, and take business needs into account as the fourth part that pulls against the other three. Short, but it's the thesis.
- **Popularity as a side effect,** including why "be original" is destructive advice.
- **The extrinsic-goals warning.** It draws on Alfie Kohn's *Punished by Rewards*, gives six effects of an extrinsic goal and a usable fix: acknowledge the need behind the goal, set it aside, then bring it back only as a secondary priority.
- **Scenarios 1 to 6, fully written.** Toxic players, cheaters, making education fun, jokes as judgements, monetization gone wrong (rich and poor players, dark patterns, the "timebombs"), and the fear that the game is too short. Scenario 6 is the most valuable practical content in the original: a catalogue of fear-driven padding (filler, artificial difficulty, arbitrary unlocks, recycling, tacked-on features, time gating, arbitrary content splitting, hidden delays, rewards for staying, punishments for leaving), each with what it is, how it's done and what it does to players. The Borderlands 2, Skyrim and Pokémon examples work.
- **The Mindset.** Purpose as the reason everything exists; NVC summarized for game design, including the dumb/smart/useless/flawed classification of strategies and the two questions that show why rewards and punishments fail; Max-Neef's matrix plus Reactorcore's own grouped needs list; turning inspiration into purpose (five steps, the OFNR sentence template, "Unmet needs + Strategy = Purpose"); creativity as remix; game vs interactive tool, including the "side-effect influence" and "If you want to make a game, make a game"; and the purpose test, "Does this fulfill the Purpose?".
- **The four stages of learning,** used to explain why the intention-vs-action mistake happens.

Weaknesses to fix while editing: Part One repeats itself at great length (the beginner/specialist contrast is argued three times at near full length, and the "why" spree runs about 40 lines), some sentences run on, spelling slips throughout ("Its", "seperate", "recieve", "lenght"), and there are dated or internal leftovers (the [THEME]/[CONTEXT] add-on system, the "Remove for HORIZON2020" note, "In 2016 and the years before").

## 3. What the articles have since corrected, extended or superseded

Most of the missing Technical Side now exists, scattered across the articles. Where an article goes beyond the original or overrules it:

**Corrections (the book should follow the article):**
- **Designer vs developer.** The original says it may use "designer" and "developer" interchangeably. *What Is a Game Designer* insists they are different jobs: in a team, the designer doesn't code, draw or market, and coders and artists end up doing two jobs when design gets dumped on them. The definitions chapter needs rewriting.
- **Scope.** The original's misc heading says to estimate the time, multiply by 8, see how long that really is, then divide the original estimate by 8 and use that as the scope limit, and "Beginners: start easy, not basic". *Scope Creep Is Not an Enemy* says make the dream game first, do "scope maximization" on paper, keep details flexible, then build the smallest playable version and grow it. They can be reconciled (maximize the plan, use the ×8 rule to size the first build), but the article's position leads. *Author note?*
- **"Design technology".** In the original it is the book's own method. *Future of Games* widens it into a field-level idea: invisible technologies like standardization and QA, which lag far behind physical technology. The widened meaning should frame the book.
- **Where needs come from.** The original says "I don't fully know who inspired who" between Max-Neef and NVC. *Games Are Need Satisfiers* states that Max-Neef coined fundamental human needs and NVC adopted them. Needs a fact-check before publication either way.
- **Competition.** The original treats competitive multiplayer neutrally in places (the beginner's list of "good" features, Scenario 1 framed as a developer's good intention). The *Forking* and *Competitive Games* articles take a total position: never design around competition, and "forking" replaces "competition" as the driver of innovation.
- **Finite games.** Scenario 7 and the misc heading "why make finite games when you can make infinite games" are superseded by *Throwaway Games*, *Infinite Metagame* and *Player-Run Services*.

**Extensions (new material the book should absorb):**
- **Self-fullness** (*Need Satisfiers*) delivers the missing "selfishness doesn't exist" explanation. The same article adds needs as independent progress bars (The Sims), opposite needs that one strategy can't serve together (relaxation vs excitement), and six ways strategies go wrong.
- **Handmade vs procedural meaning** (*Where Meaning Comes From*): the author's gift vs the player's own story.
- **Framework-driven design** (*Throwaway Games*): "Don't make a desert. Make sand, harsh sunlight and heat." This is the common mistake (final result vs root cause) applied to content. It's the strongest link between Part One and the Technical Side.
- **Foundations before content** (*How to Grow*): the dependency chain (computer performance > premise > natural laws of the game universe > environment > vehicle or character > the gun itself), Sagan's apple pie (already a heading in the original), the "crab bomb" 255-object limit as a failure of respecting technology, and buckets vs a water pipe.
- **Additive vs multiplicative content and the heuristics-driven randomizer** (*Additive vs Multiplicative*, *Future of Games*). These deliver the "Strength of Modular Design" heading.
- **Infinite metagame structure**: the infinite overarching goal plus finite local goals (*Player-Run Services*); campaign parameters as swappable modules, balancing by real-life logic (Minecraft), "perfecting your world" like a bonsai, persistent history and mementos, the regular world plus wilderness zone justified by hardware limits, and first-time, returning and expert players (*Infinite Metagame*). These deliver "Super Games/Boundless Games/Infinite Games", "Entry, Tutorials" and "Long-term usability design".
- **Concrete design rules** (*Future of Games*): no hard-coded content limits, spawn zones, structured randomness, killable story NPCs, no invisible walls, no doomed "infinite survival" modes, and a list of extrinsic motivators (unlockables, progression systems, collectables, money and shops, upgrade trees, achievement notifications).
- **The designer's job**: one designer per project, blueprints with leeway for the experts, a barebones game that's playable start to end before anything else (*Future of Games*), and six areas of mastery: human and purpose knowledge, technology and physiology, communication and business (Tad Hargrave), organization, a library of experiences, and patience and health (*What Is a Game Designer*).
- **The game starts before the main menu** (*How to Grow*): marketing, box art and store pages are part of the game experience, and can be role-played into it.
- **Abundance from the start, no grinding out of scarcity** (Big Projects tenets).
- **Universes that don't end** (*Universe 1 and 2*): a bounded "bowl" universe, worlds generated already in motion, time that never rewinds, and the player's soul respawning into a new body. These deliver the misc heading "You are immortal, make it count".
- **Personal story** (*Getting Started*, *How to Grow*): Soldat skins, then Cortex Command mods and official content, then the design awakening. This is the promised "my story" chapter.

**Tensions between the original and the articles.** Decided: wherever they conflict, the articles win (they're more recent and better worded). The ×8 rule survives only as Reactorcore's rough rule of thumb: expect everything to take longer than it seems, and use that to judge what's feasible and when to pivot to something easier. The cases:
- *One designer per project* (*Future of Games*: "too many chefs", consensus won't work) vs the original's team headings ("Compromises do not exist. Everyone *can* be satisfied.") and the third worked example, where the designer serves the partners' dream. These are reconcilable (the designer listens to everyone with NVC, then decides), but the book should say so explicitly.
- *Difficulty options*: the original's stub lists "additional extra difficulty options" as a padding trick, while *Infinite Metagame* recommends a "pro mode: no limits" toggle and early-game limiters.
- *Cosmetic monetization*: the original is "very hesitant" (status segregation, envy), while the Astroneer proposal calls cosmetics "not bad" and recommends Patreon-style support.
- *Register* (see the voice guide): the original is careful NVC, while the later articles call people "morons" and "imbeciles".

## 4. Where the bible drifted from the source

The bible reads like generic game-design advice written in Reactorcore's vocabulary. Its structure is faithful to the articles, but it misses the original's core and in several places reverses it.

**Missing (in the sources, absent from the bible):**
- Game vs interactive tool, gamification as manipulation, the side-effect influence and "if you want to make a game, make a game". This is the original's most emphatic rule.
- The extrinsic-goals warning (making games primarily for money or fame).
- Rewards and punishments as two sides of one coin, and the two questions that expose them.
- The whole fear-driven padding catalogue (time gating, hidden delays, content splitting, rewards for staying, punishments for leaving). Only artificial difficulty survives, in 14.2.
- Question Everything and its trap list, popularity as a side effect, the four stages of learning.
- The partner interview kit, the jokes-as-judgements scenario, OFNR, "Unmet needs + Strategy = Purpose", the dumb/smart/useless/flawed strategies, creativity as remix.
- Designer ≠ developer, one designer per project, design including marketing (8.1 touches it), the foundations-first dependency chain, framework-driven design, the wilderness zone and hardware limits, mementos, time never rewinding, the two-layer goal (5.4 has the service framing but not the overarching/local goal structure), abundance from the start, scope maximization.

**Distorted (the bible says the opposite of the source):**
- **Educational and life-enriching games.** 7.2 tells designers to "include educational elements" and "inspire real-world action", and 5.2 and 12.2 list educational games and platforms as fitting structures. The original's central rule is that any intent of real-life consequence turns a game into a tool, and that disguising it kills the game.
- **Achievements and praise.** 6.3, 7.2, 7.4, 8.3, 9.3 and 13.4 recommend "achievement celebration moments", "positive achievement systems", "celebrate progress", "reward collaboration" and "rewards for teaching". The original calls achievements "doggy biscuit rewards", condemns hollow praise ("Good Job!", "You're Awesome!") and asks for "gratitude & celebrate successes (not praise/compliments!)". The articles list achievement notifications as extrinsic motivators and say the history display must "never be used to reward nor punish".
- **Monetization.** 14.3 suggests "genuine limited content (seasonal events) or fair rotating availability". The original's time-gating section names exactly these, event-exclusive content and rotating shop inventory, as manipulation. It also offers paid "faster travel" and "automation" as convenience features, when the original lists removing conveniences such as fast travel as a padding trick, so selling them back is the pattern it warns about.
- **Adaptive difficulty.** 6.3 recommends performance-monitoring dynamic difficulty. *Infinite Metagame* attacks scaling opponents and zones ("I am powerful because fuck you") and recommends swappable campaign modules and real-life-logic balancing instead. 6.3 also has "mandatory rest periods", which goes against the autonomy the source protects (the robot example only asks for reminders and the real-world time).
- **The ×8 rule.** 12.4 turns it into a deadline multiplier (beginners ×8, experienced ×5, plus invented team and new-tech multipliers and resource-allocation percentages). In the source the ×8 is used to *shrink the scope*, not to stretch the timeline.
- **Competing vs forking.** 15.2 gives a "when to compete" checklist (market validation, resource advantage) and redefines forking as one competitive strategy among others. The article replaces competition with forking entirely.
- **Toxicity.** 9.3 says to "address toxic behavior quickly and consistently". The source says rules and bans can't fix a structural cause (Scenario 1), and that banning makes it worse (Scenario 5). The bible contradicts itself here (14.1 gets it right).
- **Scarcity.** 13.1 ("resource competition: create meaningful choices through scarcity", "Summer: peak competition periods") sits against the abundance tenet. Scarcity in the source is only ever a natural consequence or a campaign parameter, never a design goal.
- **Small games.** 12.2 recommends small games for "learning projects" and "portfolio pieces" and a staged v1/v2/v3 path. *Throwaway Games* says the age of small games is ending, and *Scope Creep* rejects "make copies first as training". (The bible's preamble, "not every project requires super game treatment", is a deliberate pipeline instruction and probably should stay in the bible, but it isn't the book's position.)

**Invented (no source I could find):**
- "Waterfall after planning", "the architect approach", "every project is always purpose-built" and "the lock-down moment" (3.1–3.4). They aren't in the original or any article. *Scope Creep* says the opposite on one point (keep the details flexible; "good design is paranoid design"). Reactorcore confirmed there's no other source, so they're the earlier model's invention and stay out of the book. The real idea underneath, that everything in a game exists to serve its purpose, is already in chapters 8 and 13.
- Learning styles (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, reading learners) in 7.4 and 8.3. That's a debunked theory and not Reactorcore's.
- The "5-minute rule", "user testimonials", the kill/pivot/continue checklists, backcasting steps, forest-ecosystem, predator-prey, mycelium and seasons models, chaos percentages (1/5/10/15%) and theme odds (40/30/20/10%), and the feelings-to-needs translation table. Some are harmless filler, but none of it is Reactorcore's thinking.
- The note in 3.3 about 2D sprite art being fine is a pipeline instruction pasted into a chapter, not book content.

Reactorcore's verdict: the bible is contaminated by the earlier model's training data and gets discarded. D9 writes a new one from the finished book rather than revising the old one.

## 6. Voice guide

The book is Reactorcore talking to one reader, a designer who already has a burning reason to make games. It sounds like an experienced friend who has made every mistake in it, is completely sure of what they've learned, and wants to save you the ten years.

**Signature devices (keep all of them):**
- **Slash clusters** pin one meaning with several words: "vision/inspiration/fantasy/dream/wish/idea", "free/powered/energetic/focused/serious/alive/aggressive", "Masking/pretending/faking". Use two to four words normally; long chains are for lists of sources ("game/movie/music/comic/book/nature/event"). Use about one per few paragraphs, not one per sentence. They're allowed in headings, as in the needs list ("Safety/Protection/Peace/Order").
- **Restatement openers**: "In other words…", "Basically…", "To explain it in a different way…", "To put it simply:", "Said in a more plain language…". The pattern is an abstract claim, then the same claim concrete. Keep both halves; the original promises this explicitly.
- **First person and direct address.** "I" for experience and feelings, "you" for warnings and instructions. Polite asks: "I ask you to keep this in mind", "Please do your best to avoid it", "Thank you."
- **Feelings stated as feelings.** "I feel sad because…", "I feel disgusted whenever I see a developer try this trick", "This makes me feel worried, as I'm not fully sure…". This is the NVC voice, and it's what lets the book be harsh about practices without judging people.
- **Plain-spoken certainty.** "guaranteed", "always", "Nothing more, nothing less.", "Hah. No. Absolutely not.", "It does not get better than this!", "That path leads to ruin, do not take it." No hedging, and no "it depends" unless the purpose really decides it.
- **Short hammer lines after long sentences.** "Garbage. Junk." "Nobody wins. Everyone loses." "Small steps." "Its never worth it."
- **Anticipating the reader's objection.** "But wait! You might ask this next:", "Now, this might sound a bit absurd…", and clarifying notes that start "Please note that this is not to be confused with…".
- **Labelled scaffolding.** Unaware Beginner vs Experienced Specialist; Story / Action / Problem / Intention / Mistake; (Name of the action) / (What is it) / (How to do it) / (What are its effects); question sprees. Keep these formats, since they're how the book teaches.
- **Homely analogies.** Rocket engineering, x-ray vision, the compass, the square peg, the dog chasing its tail, contaminated water, the carrot on a stick, the edible elephant, the satellite, buckets vs a pipe, polishing a ball of crap, a cake turned into soup, the bonsai. Add new ones in this style: everyday, physical, slightly funny.
- **Named real games** as evidence (Borderlands 2, Skyrim, Pokémon, Minecraft, RuneScape, Deus Ex, Fallout: New Vegas, The Sims, Factorio, Soldat, Cortex Command).
- **Dry humour.** "You may receive a fist in the face if you try it." "Poor Elmo didn't survive either." "Hahaha… ha… whoops."
- **Humility about the reader.** "I do not trust that this information will fully protect you from even making one mistake." "Good luck and stay smart."
- **Universal scope markers.** "and I mean that in every context you can think of", "any, and I mean ANY". Caps for the few load-bearing words (PRIMARY, SECONDARY, NEVER) become `<strong>` in HTML, sparingly. `*asterisk*` emphasis becomes `<em>`.
- **Recurring phrases**: "make life more wonderful", "fundamental human needs", "root cause", "the common mistake", "throwaway", "Does this serve the purpose?", "message sent = message received", "question everything", "everything that exists, exists for a reason".

**Editing rules:**
- Fix spelling and grammar ("it's", "separate", "receive", "length"), and split the worst run-on sentences. Keep the rhythm, the word choices and the slight non-native flavour ("Thing is,", "Lets say", "So thats that."). Don't smooth it into corporate prose.
- Cut repetition, not voice. When the original says something three times, keep the best version and one restatement.
- When an article overrules the original, rewrite the passage to the article's position in the original's voice, and mark it `<!-- Author note? … -->` if the change is substantive.
- No AI-isms: no "Core Principle:" labels, bold-led bullet frameworks, "Implementation Guidelines", invented percentages, "delve", or triplets for rhythm. The bible is the example of what *not* to sound like.
- Fact-check quotes before publishing. The Einstein line is probably apocryphal, the Sagan line needs its exact wording, and the Max-Neef → NVC lineage needs confirming. The CNVC PDF links need checking too.
- Remove the suggestion to torrent NVC material (the book will be sold), the "Remove for HORIZON2020" note, and dated references.
- Identity: no real name, and no mention of the home country. "A peaceful country" is fine.

**The register decision (decided: the original's register).** The original is careful NVC: it forbids saying the labels out loud, calls judgements violence, and says jokes always need a victim. The later articles are much harsher ("incompetent morons", "fuck you and listen up", "dogshit"). A book that teaches that labels are violence can't call its reader an imbecile without undercutting itself. My recommendation: use the original's register as the base. Be blunt and angry about *practices and ideas* (padding is "a dirty little trick", the scope-creep warning is "a false boogeyman"), state your feelings, never label *people*, and keep profanity rare and aimed at practices, as the original does ("advertise the fuck out of it", "this bullshit treatment").
