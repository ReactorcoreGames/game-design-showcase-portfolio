Game overview

Colossal Devastators is a 2D platform brawler where you're the monster of the week. You play a 20-meter alien titan sent to conquer a modern world: stomp through its cities, shrug off its armies, and fight the prototype super robots it builds to stop you.

It plays like Rampage crossed with Super Smash Bros: simple Smash-style controls and platforming, but a plain 100-point health bar instead of knock-out percentages, and a slow, heavy pace where the big attacks take time to wind up and the buildings around you are both cover and debris.

The real draw is the builder. Any giant you can think of, robot, cyborg or beast, can be made into a fighter, and the game balances it for you from three simple dials. It grows out of two PC freeware favorites, Multihero (a sprite-based Smash Bros) and Divinoids (a Rampage remake), with the slow, deliberate ability play of the Pokémon RPGs and the super robot shows of the seventies onwards.

Target audience

People who grew up rooting for the monster in Godzilla, Voltes V or the Power Rangers' Megazord fights, and who loved Rampage and Smash Bros for how easy they were to pick up.

They want to feel huge, and to design the giant they always wished someone would make.

Fighting games' frame data and long combo strings put them off. Here the controls are Smash-simple and the pace is slow enough to think in.

Game's purpose

Power and silliness: being the 20-meter thing the whole army is shooting at, and the city falling over around you.

Creation: building any giant you can imagine, and seeing it play fairly.

Excitement: titan duels where one charged blow lands like a building coming down.

It avoids draining clarity and rest: simple controls, a plain health bar, and attacks big enough to see coming.

Core concept

Fighting games usually ship a fixed roster, balanced by hand, with every new character a costly project.

Here the roster is a builder: three dials set a giant's strengths and costs, and a handful of standardized sprites and effects make each new one cheap to add.

Rampage's mindless smashing gets a fighting game's duels at the end, and the invasion has a whole world to work through.

Lore and story

The other side of the show

Every super robot show has an alien empire that sends a new giant monster down every week, and a plucky defense force that builds the robot to stop it. This game is that show from the empire's side. You're the monster.

It's the excuse the game needs to let you knock down buildings and blow things up, in the spirit of Voltes V, the Power Rangers' Megazord fights, Godzilla and Pacific Rim, with the cast swapped around.

The locals

The world you're conquering has modern technology and a puny conventional army: tanks, jets, helicopters, warships and the occasional megatank. As in the shows, the sirens send the people into shelters before you land, so it's the army, the buildings and the bridges that stand in your way.

Every so often the locals pull out something special: a prototype giant robot of their own, or a massive superweapon structure guarding the city. Those are the real fights.

The job right now

Land, wreck, and beat whatever the defenders throw at you, one city at a time, until the planet gives in. There's always another city, and another world after this one.

Controls and interface

You control one giant on a side-on stage, with keys to move and the mouse to aim, the way Smash Bros' simplified sprite cousins play. Every titan uses the same eight inputs; what each one does depends on the build.

The four attack buttons don't have to be attacks: a module can just as well be a protective move, a heal or a buff. The health bar is 100 points for everyone, and the screen shows the energy, ammunition, cooldowns or heat each of your abilities runs on.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    A giant in a small world

    You walk, jump and swing through a side-on stage that's mostly city: towers, trees, bridges, props and swarms of tiny vehicles. Everything breaks. Tanks plink at your shins, jets dive at your head, and you decide what to flatten on the way.

    When a rival titan shows up, the stage becomes an arena. You trade slow, heavy blows, duck behind a tower while their beam charges, and step out to land your own.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A city's defenses

    A campaign is one city, played as a run of adventure stages in the style of Multihero's adventure mode: gauntlets of lesser enemies, a recurring nemesis or two, hazard courses to cross, and a boss at the end, either the city's prototype robot or the superweapon guarding it.

    Beat the boss and the city is yours. It's a complete arc with a start, a climax and an end, and the next city is a new one.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The invasion

    The planet map remembers every city you've taken, and the defenders remember you: a prototype robot that escaped comes back rebuilt in another city, and the nemesis enemies you keep meeting get history with you.

    When one world has fallen, the empire points you at the next, with its own cities, armies and prototypes. Your hangar of titans stays with you the whole way, every one of them built by you, so the invasion is also the story of the monsters you made.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    One duel or one city

    Start a single arena battle for a ten-minute duel, or pick up the invasion map and play the next stage of the city you're working on. Either way you're in a fight within a minute.

    Stop after any stage and the invasion waits where you left it. The builder is there between fights for as long, or as little, as you like.

Game modes

ModeWhat it's for
InvasionThe adventure campaign: cities of platforming gauntlets, rampage stages and boss fights against the defenders' prototypes, across one world after another
ArenaA single titan duel on an arena stage, against computer-controlled giants or a friend who fancies a play-fight, for when you want just the fight or to try out a new build

Both use the same titans, controls and stages. The invasion is where the game lives; the arena is a sandbox for the builder.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    A builder that balances itself

    Any character from any game or show you've ever wanted to play as can be rebuilt here, on one condition: it's always a 20-meter giant robot, cyborg or beast. You don't hand-tune its numbers. You set three dials, and the game works out the rest:

    DialWhat it meansWhat it shapes
    MassHow big and heavy the titan isHealth and staying power, at the cost of mobility
    SophisticationHow advanced its body and systems areVersatility: transformations, modules, how many tricks it has
    Output powerHow much energy it can throw aroundDamage, and how hard its abilities lean on ammo, cooldowns or heat

    Health, damage, mobility and versatility all follow from those three, so a lumbering fortress, a fragile glass cannon and a nimble trickster can all exist side by side and all be fair. Some titans run on ammunition, some on cooldowns, some on a heat meter; some transform and some don't.

  2. Two bodies in one

    Mech form and beast form

    Many titans have two forms and switch between them with one key: usually a humanoid mech and a mythical beast, though nothing says it has to be those two. Each form moves differently and has its own attacks, so switching mid-fight is a real decision, not a costume change.

    A dragon-mech might fly and breathe fire as a beast and stand and shoot as a robot. A titan without a second form gets more out of its single one instead.

  3. Slow and heavy

    Delayed devastating attacks

    The fighting borrows the feel of the Pokémon RPGs: preset abilities that attack, defend, strengthen yourself or weaken the other side, each running on rechargeable energy, with the deliberate pace of turn-based combat played out in real time.

    The biggest attacks take a long time to wind up and hit like the end of an episode. That makes the city part of the fight: a building between you and a charging beam is cover, and knocking it down to get at your opponent is a move of its own.

    The health system is the old Street Fighter II bar, not Smash Bros' percentages: everyone has 100 points, damage takes them away, and at zero your titan explodes. No ring-outs, no being knocked off the screen by a lucky hit.

  4. Fit it out

    Augments, modules and a super

    On top of its body, every titan carries three augments that enhance its melee or ranged attacks, and two ability modules on Q and E. One more key fires its super ability, and its defensive move is its own: a dodge, a shield, a counter, whatever fits the build.

    All of it is open from the start in the builder. There's nothing to collect or wait for; the only question is which combination makes the titan you have in mind.

  5. Rampage

    A world that breaks

    Buildings, trees, bridges and props all come down, as in Rampage and Divinoids, and the army fights back with tanks, jets, warships, megatanks and small units scattered around your feet.

    The adventure stages add the stage hazards of the Game Boy Advance Mario and Sonic games, scaled up to a giant: crumbling platforms, pits, crushers and moving machinery to get across between the fights.

  6. Cheap to make, endless to add

    A giant in four poses

    The builder only works if new titans are cheap, so the art is built on standards. Each titan needs just a few sprites, idle, move, attack and jump, at 64 to 128 pixels, with hitboxes that follow the shape of the drawing.

    Everything else is shared and tinted: standard hit effects in three weights (light, smash and super), one orb shield, a universal beam system in the style of Cryptark's lasers, and common fire, gas, plasma, explosions and lightning that any titan can use in its own colors. Only non-beam projectiles need art of their own.

    That makes a new titan a small job, even for someone with modest art skills, and each one added gives every stage, mode and rival something new to fight.

This week's monster

Colossal Devastators: Kaiju Rampage Brawler

The sirens are wailing. The shelters are full. The army has rolled out every tank it owns, and somewhere under the city a team of scientists is bolting the last armor plate onto their brand-new super robot.

None of it is ready for what you've built. Land, roar, and give them an episode to remember.

You're the one they write the theme song against.
