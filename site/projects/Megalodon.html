Megalodon FirefighterLight-Gun Fire Rescue

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Megalodon Firefighter plays like a light-gun arcade game, Time Crisis or Virtua Cop with a crosshair on the screen, except that nobody's shooting at you. The danger is to the scene: a city block on fire, seen from the side, with more burning debris still falling from the sky.

The fire is a falling-sand simulation: every pixel of wood, stone, lava and water follows a few simple rules, so a fire spreads, heats the stone next to it, melts it into flowing lava, and keeps going until something stops it. You're the gunner of the Megalodon, a huge azure mecha-helicopter built for firefighting, with a water cannon to put fires out and an autocannon to shoot down meteors before they land.

The goal of every stage is the same: save as much as you can. It's a short, focused game in the spirit of the best Flash-era web games, built on the rules of falling-sand toys like The Powder Toy.

Target audience

People who lose hours watching falling-sand toys burn and flow, and people who loved pointing a light gun at an arcade screen.

Falling-sand sandboxes give them the simulation but no reason to play it; light-gun games give them the reason but only ever as shooting someone.

The game is played with the mouse alone, and a crisis fits in an evening.

Game's purpose

Care and protection: the satisfaction of saving things instead of destroying them.

Mastery: learning how fire, heat and lava really move, and where one well-aimed blast stops a whole chain.

Enjoyment: the simple beauty of a simulation where water hisses into steam and lava cools into black glass.

It avoids draining rest (short stages, a generous water supply) and clarity (four materials, each with rules you can see working).

Core concept

Falling-sand games are usually sandboxes with no goal, and light-gun games are on-rails shooting galleries.

This one puts a clear, finite job on top of a simulation: one crisis, nine stages, as little damage as possible.

Your best tool against meteors also starts fires, so every shot is a judgment call, not a reflex.

Lore and story

The Megalodon

The Megalodon is one of a kind: a big, bulky, heavily armored hybrid of mecha and helicopter, painted azure and built for turbo firefighting. It carries a water cannon fed from an enormous ultra-compressed reserve tank, and an autocannon for shooting falling debris out of the sky or blasting obstacles out of the way.

The rupture

On one mission, a rupture in space-time opened, and the Megalodon fell into it. It made it back out, but not cleanly. It's now tangled up with that rupture: every so often it warps to a different time and place, and it can't choose where or when.

The one thing that's always the same is where it lands: somewhere a shower of meteorites or space wreckage is about to set a town on fire. The tangle that keeps it from going home also gives it the chance to change how those days turned out.

Fires across history

Each crisis is a different place in a different era, generated fresh: its own skyline of buildings, its own colors of timber and stone, its own shower of fire coming down from above.

The job right now

Hot debris has already landed, and the first fires have a head start. More is on its way down. Put out what's burning, stop the heat from melting the stone, shoot down what you can, and keep as much of this town standing as possible before the warp takes you away.

Controls and interface

You don't fly the Megalodon. You're its gunner, looking at the scene from the side the way a light-gun player looks at the screen, and the mouse is your crosshair. Everything happens where you point.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    A burning block

    One side-view scene: three to seven generated buildings standing on the ground, with small burning pieces of debris already scattered across them. You read where the fire is spreading, which walls are getting hot, and what's still coming down, and decide where the next shot goes.

    A blast of water on a burning wall puts it out. A blast on hot stone cools it before it melts. A meteor caught in the crosshair can be shot down, if the shot won't do more harm than the meteor.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A crisis in nine stages

    A crisis is one disaster: a single meteor shower whose nine clusters come down on nine parts of the same area, one per stage. Each stage ends when the fire is out, and its result is what survived.

    If the water reserve runs dry while something is still burning, that stage is lost and the area burns down. The crisis goes on to the next stage, and the result keeps what happened.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The warp and the log

    When a crisis is over, the warp takes the Megalodon somewhere else, and there's always another fire. Every crisis you've flown goes into a log: where and when it was, and how much of it you saved.

    Each entry keeps the seed it was generated from, so you can fly the same crisis again, building for building and meteor for meteor, and see if you can save more of it this time, like retaking an exam you know you can do better.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A stage over coffee

    A stage lasts a few minutes, a crisis about an evening. Between stages is the place to stop: the crisis waits there for you. The game starts in the log, so the next crisis or an old one to retake is a click away.

Game modes

There's one mode, the warp: crisis after crisis in different times and places, with any old one available to fly again from the log.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Two guns, one judgment call

    The water cannon is the firefighting tool. Both its rapid shots and its charged blast burst in a circle on impact: anything burning inside it goes out at once, and the burst sprays a ring of real water pixels that keep flowing and putting out fires until they're used up or evaporate.

    The autocannon is for meteors and obstacles: rapid armor-piercing explosive rounds, or a charged electromagnetic shot for the rarer, harder meteors and anything that blocks the water.

    That's the dilemma. A meteor shot in mid-air turns to harmless ash. But a miss lands in the town, and autocannon shells can set fires of their own, worse than the meteor would have. Sometimes the right call is to let it land and put out what it starts.

  2. Falling sand, kept small

    Four materials, simple rules

    Everything on the screen is made of four materials, each with a handful of rules. That's enough for fires that spread, walls that melt and water that runs, and few enough that a player can learn all of them:

    MaterialHow it behaves
    WoodStays put. Catches fire from burning neighbors at about one pixel a second, burns for five minutes, then turns to ash. While burning, it heats any stone touching it.
    StoneStays put and collects heat. At 100 heat it melts into lava, which flows, heats the stone it touches, and cools into inert black obsidian after ten minutes.
    WaterFalls and flows sideways. Puts out burning wood, cools hot stone, and turns lava into obsidian, using itself up each time. Washes ash away without being used up. Evaporates after a minute.
    TerrainThe ground itself: immune to everything. Water, ash and lava pile up on it.

    Burning wood flickers with flames, and lava gives off rising smoke, so you can read the state of the fire at a glance.

  3. Break the chain early

    Heat that travels

    The real danger isn't the flames you can see. It's the heat they pass into the stone, because melted stone turns into lava that runs downhill and heats more stone wherever it goes:

    1. Burning woodHeats the stone it touches, 10 heat a second
    2. Hot stoneMelts into lava at 100 heat
    3. LavaFlows, and heats more stone, 2 heat a second
    4. ObsidianAfter ten minutes, inert black glass

    Water breaks the chain at every link: it puts out the wood, resets any stone above 20 heat back to zero, and freezes lava into obsidian on the spot. A quick blast on a warming wall early is worth a dozen shots on a lava flow later.

  4. From anywhere, not just above

    Meteors you see coming at you

    New debris falls during the firefighting, and it comes in toward the screen, the way targets do in a light-gun game, aimed at any spot in the scene rather than dropping in from the top edge. You see it grow and have a moment to decide.

    Shot down in flight, it bursts into ash that keeps its momentum, plus a push outward from the hit. Allowed to land, it goes off like a cluster bomb, spraying lava pixels from where it hit.

  5. Every town is new

    Generated buildings

    Buildings are drawn by a simple generator: one or more large rectangles for the body, then smaller rectangles and horizontal and vertical lines added by if/else rules for floors, beams, balconies and wiring. It can make anything from a small hut to a wide warehouse or a tall, strange tower, always connected to the ground.

    Each stage picks one color for its wood and one for its stone: wood in browns, tans, greens, yellows or blues (timber, cable, insulation), stone in darker, duller greys, reds or whites (brick, concrete, metal), and the ground darker still. Every town looks like its own place.

  6. Spray freely

    A generous, finite reserve

    The Megalodon's reserve tank is ultra-compressed and huge. You can spray as much as you like, refilling the active tank whenever you want, and for most of a stage you won't think about it.

    It's not infinite, though, and that keeps the water honest: a stage that's sprayed carelessly at the wrong fires can still run dry before the last wall is out. Water is the limit that the game's whole fail state hangs on, and it's shown plainly the whole time.

Dispatch log, date unknown

Megalodon Firefighter: Light-Gun Fire Rescue

Warp complete. Location: unknown. Year: unknown. Debris on the ground and burning, more inbound from above. Reserve tank full. Water cannon ready. Autocannon ready, use with care.

Nobody here asked for us, and nobody here will remember we came. But the town will still be standing tomorrow, more of it than there would have been.

Wherever it burns. Whenever it burns.
