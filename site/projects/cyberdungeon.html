Cyberdungeon RiderMegacorp Agent Motocross

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Cyberdungeon Rider is a 2D motocross game built on Elastomania's bike physics, where the rider and the bike are one android. At the press of a key he folds up from a motorbike into a combat robot and back, to cross a generated corporate course from the start line to its finish: a reactor with a vulnerable core.

The bike leans, wheelies and lands the way Elastomania's does, but a crash into the scenery costs health instead of ending the run. The robot walks, jumps, fights and flips gravity to run along the ceiling. Land the killing blow on the reactor, and the course ends with the android racing away on two wheels as the installation goes up behind him.

It's inspired by Elastomania above all, by the finite courses of Nate Adams Freestyle Motocross, by the rider-agent of the manga Biomega, and by the zoomed-out view of Soldat, with the look of Canabalt and Butcher.

Target audience

Players who lost evenings to Elastomania, Trials or a Flash motocross game, chasing the perfect lean over a hill and the clean landing after it.

They loved the feel of the bike and hated restarting the whole level because a helmet brushed a rock.

It's keyboard and mouse, and slow motion is one key away whenever a jump or a shot needs more time.

Game's purpose

Mastery and movement: the bodily joy of balancing a bike across rough ground and getting better at it.

Excitement: a ride that builds to a reactor going up behind you.

Meaning: pushing on to the finish line while a rival does everything to make you doubt you'll get there.

It avoids draining safety and rest: a health bar instead of instant failure, and slow motion on demand.

Core concept

Physics bike games usually come as a fixed pack of handmade levels, each a run of trial and error where one touch of the head means starting again.

Here every course is generated, mistakes cost health rather than the run, and a second body for climbing and fighting turns a pure obstacle course into a raid.

There's always another installation to bring down.

Lore and story

The cyberdungeon

In a future of runaway megacorporations, a few nefarious organizations are trying to take over the world by evil means. Their installations sprawl across whole regions: hills cut with service roads, caves full of cables, concrete halls and the reactors that power it all, watched by cameras and patrolled by drones.

The rider

Kaze is a corporate agent android, and a guardian rider in the mold of a nineties anime hero: he is both the motorbike and the robot riding it. His job is to take those organizations down one region at a time, by riding into each installation and destroying its reactor, which shuts it down for good.

The ghost

Cyberghost is his rival, an agent of an evil corporation who haunts every course as a flickering racing ghost. He rides alongside, taunts, and tries to make Kaze give up while he's fighting droids and turrets on the way to the reactor.

He's the game's heart in disguise: the doubts, the haters and the mishaps that turn up whenever someone chases a dream. The answer to him is the same as the answer to the course: keep riding and reach the finish line anyway.

The job right now

Get through this installation, put the reactor down, and outrun the blast. Then the next one.

Controls and interface

The camera is centered on Kaze and zoomed well out, as in Soldat, with a small rider and plenty of the course in view. Each mode has its own movement keys; transforming, aiming, shooting and slow motion work the same in both.

Slow motion can be switched on at any moment, for as long as you like: to make a landing, to line up a shot, or just to watch a jump in style.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    One course, start to reactor

    A course is a finite ride with a start line and a finish. You lean and throttle over long hilly stretches, thread curves and caves, and switch to the robot for the concrete interiors, the climbs and the fights.

    Cameras shoot, drones close in, and Cyberghost flickers past with something cutting to say. At the end waits the reactor, and the course ends the moment its core takes the killing blow.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A region's installations

    A campaign takes on one organization's hold on a region: a string of generated courses, each one an installation with its own reactor. Reaching each finish line is what matters above all.

    At every finish the game celebrates how you rode: your time, the damage you took and the damage you dealt, as a moment shared with you rather than a grade. When the last reactor in the region is down, the organization is out of it for good.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    Bringing them down

    The world map shows where each organization still has a grip, and goes dark region by region as you shut their installations down. There are always more regions and more schemers trying the same thing, so there's always another campaign.

    The map is the record of every reactor you've put out, and a picture of how far Kaze has come in spite of everything Cyberghost said along the way.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    One ride at a time

    Launch, pick up the campaign you're in, and you're on the start line. A course takes a few minutes, and the reactor going up is a natural place to stop.

    The campaign waits between courses for as long as you're away, and saves on the way through each one, so stopping mid-ride doesn't cost you the course.

Game modes

There's one mode, the campaign, built from pieces you can stop between:

The worldEndless: organization after organization, region after region.
A regionOne campaign: a string of installations, an evening or two.
A courseOne installation, start line to reactor: a few minutes.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Two bodies, one rider

    Kaze switches freely between a motorbike and a humanoid robot, and every course is built to need both. Each is good where the other isn't:

    ModeBest atThe gun
    BikeCrossing wide stretches fast, and plowing through lesser obstacles and droids that would stop the robotA turret on the bike, aimed with the mouse while you ride
    RobotFighting, platforming and fast vertical travel: climbs, shafts, ceilingsA big hand-held pistol

    The gun is the same electromagnetic accelerator in both modes. It just changes shape with the body, so switching never leaves you unarmed.

  2. Elastomania, forgiven

    Bike physics with a health bar

    The bike is the heart of the game, and it handles like Elastomania's: two springy wheels and a body you shift with lean back and lean forward. Throttle too hard and you wheelie; lean wrong on a crest and you land on your back wheel or your face. Turning around is a single key, so you can ride back up a slope you just came down.

    What's different is what happens when it goes wrong. In Elastomania, the rider's head touching anything ends the attempt. Here a bad landing or a scrape against the scenery takes health, so mistakes are allowed, and you can shrug off a few on the way to getting it right.

  3. Up is a choice

    Gravity flip and the grappling hook

    In robot mode, Space turns Kaze upside down and reverses his gravity, so he falls up to the ceiling and runs along it. A shaft that would take a long climb takes one keypress, and a corridor full of floor hazards has a whole second floor overhead.

    The grappling hook works in both modes, on its own mouse button: swing the robot across a gap, or pull the bike up a lip it couldn't ride.

  4. Plow or shoot

    Security in the way

    The combat is light, and it's there to make the ride a raid, not to replace it. The installations defend themselves with security cameras carrying a rudimentary gun, and slow, FPV-style drones with guns or explosive mines hanging under them.

    The bike can plow straight through the ones that are safe to hit, and the robot's pistol takes them down at range. The mine carriers are the ones you don't want to touch, so they get shot first, on the move from the bike's turret or standing still as the robot. Outdoors and indoors each have their own hazards and targets, so the choice between plow and shoot keeps changing.

  5. A new ride every time

    Generated courses

    No course is handmade. Each one is generated from the same few pieces, in the spirit of a track editor that drops in terrain, obstacles, props, checkpoint lines, a start and a finish:

    1. OutdoorsLong stretches of hilly terrain and curves, for the bike
    2. CavesTunnels that twist up and down, for both
    3. InteriorsRectangular concrete sections, for the robot
    4. The reactorThe finish line, always last

    Every section has its own hazards and its own targets, so a course mixes riding, climbing and fighting differently each time, and the one after it is never the same.

  6. The finish line

    The reactor and the getaway

    Every course ends at a big reactor with a vulnerable core. It's the finish line and the last fight in one: get to it, break through to the core, and land the killing blow.

    That's the moment the installation is shut down for good, and the moment the game hands you its best scene: Kaze, back in bike mode, racing away from the blast as the installation goes up in a huge explosion behind him. It's the end of every course, and it never gets old.

Rider dispatch

Cyberdungeon Rider: Megacorp Agent Motocross

Hills, caves, concrete and cameras, all the way down to a reactor they think nobody can reach. A ghost on your shoulder telling you you'll never make it.

Lean into the crest. Fold into the robot, flip, climb, fold back. Reach the core, hit it, and ride.

Don't look back at the explosion.
