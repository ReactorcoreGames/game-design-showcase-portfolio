Thunderstrike JusticeArcade Air Combat

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Thunderstrike Justice is an arcade air-combat game about one plain, cathartic fantasy: flying a fighter jet straight at the people who make the world worse, and wiping their machinery off the map. Ten generated strikes, one tyrant at the end of them, and nothing in your way but their own clumsy army.

It's built on the streamlined flying of the mobile game Fighters!, realistic in look but simple to fly, with the premise of Gundam 00's Celestial Being, a secret force that topples the cruel by force, and low-poly art in the spirit of Star Fox 64.

Its bigger sibling, Starforge Legion, takes the same foundations into space with jets that transform into mecha. Thunderstrike keeps to jets, sea and sky.

Target audience

Players who want a quick, loud, satisfying flight game: fans of Fighters!, Star Fox 64 or the strike missions of Ace Combat, and anyone who has read the news and wished they could do something about it.

They're hungry for a clean sense of justice done, and put off by flight sims that need a manual and by shooters that make them aim at people like themselves.

It plays on the keyboard alone, like Fighters!: throttle, steer, fire, afterburner, switch weapon, and a button that turns the camera to the nearest enemy. Lock-on is automatic.

Game's purpose

Catharsis and power: ending the reign of someone cruel, in the most obvious way possible, where in real life ordinary people can do nothing about them.

Excitement: strafing runs, missile locks, burning wreckage and a slow-motion finale.

It deliberately avoids draining rest (stages take minutes), clarity (no stealth, every enemy on the radar) and respect: the enemies are fictional archetypes built from cruelty and greed, never a real country or people.

Core concept

Air-combat games usually script a finite campaign of someone else's war: you fly their missions once, and it's over.

Here every campaign is ten generated stages ending at a generated tyrant, and there's always another one. Winning is normal. The tension comes from a campaign that can be lost for good, and a target who walks free if it is.

Lore and story

The squadron

A secret international squadron, answerable to no government, has built a handful of fighter jets more advanced than anything the world's regimes can put in the air. It's called Thunderstrike, and its pilots have one job: find the people in power who are hurting everyone below them, and bring them down by destroying the machinery that keeps them there.

Nobody else will. The people with the power to stop them are too often protected, too comfortable, or quietly profiting.

The regimes

The world is a fictional one, much like ours: seas, islands, deserts and coasts, and states run by people who've grown too comfortable. Their militaries guard the infrastructure of cruelty: prison camps and black sites, surveillance centers, propaganda studios, poison-drug factories, refineries poisoning the coast, ghost cities built for profit, the walled enclaves of the elite, and the dual-use factories that serve all of them.

None of them is a real country. They're defined by what they do, not by who lives there.

The targets

Each campaign names one person. The game generates a name and a two-word profile, a flaw and a crime: a sociopath slaver, a narcissist dictator, an apathetic thug, a greedy executive, a manipulative cult leader. Their portrait is a vague silhouette in a soft tint, because they aren't anyone in particular, only what someone becomes when power meets that flaw.

The job right now

Ten strikes, then the target. Erase the name, and go and find the next one.

Controls and interface

The jet flies like the jets in Fighters!: always forward, between a minimum and a maximum speed. Turns are wide at speed and tighter when you slow down, so you attack ground targets in strafing runs and fight other jets by chasing their tails. There's no mouse aiming; the keys below are the whole game.

The HUD shows your jet's condition, the current weapon and its ammunition, speed, altitude, a compass with your position, and the kill list of targets left. The radar in the corner marks you in green and enemies in red: a dot for one at your altitude, an arrow for one above or below. The current target wears a red box that shrinks to a dot when it's far, and when it's off-screen an arrow at the edge shows the fastest way to turn toward it. "Remaining" changes to "all clear" when the stage is done.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    A strike in a ten-kilometer cube

    Every stage is a cube of sky ten kilometers wide. You enter already flying, from one side of the cube or diving in from above, and three seconds later comes "all systems go" as the targeting locks onto the nearest enemy.

    Some targets fly and some sit on the ground. Some never shoot back; others fire crude shots and try to fly at you. You clear them one by one: a strafing run along a line of tanks, a tail chase after a jet, a wide loop to come around for another pass.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    Ten stages to the target

    A campaign opens on its target's name, profile and silhouette. Then comes a row of ten boxes, an arrow on the one you're about to enter, and a button to start the first battle. Each stage has its own random difficulty, so the curve can dip and climb, and the target waits in the tenth.

    If your jet goes down, the mission has failed and you're back at the start screen. A campaign lost on its first run is gone for good: it never enters the win log, and its seed is skipped from then on. The target got away, and the next campaign is a new one.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The win log

    The win log keeps every campaign you've won: its name and total time, and each stage's time within it. You can replay a whole campaign or a single stage as a flashback, from the same seed, and a faster run improves its time and the campaign's total.

    In a flashback the target is already dead and buried, so they appear as a faceless shadow with a garbled name. A flashback is purely for the satisfaction of flying that particular challenge better. The log is a trophy wall you can polish if you want to, and that's all it is.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A stage between other things

    The start screen has everything in one place: a new game, the current campaign, the win log, how to play, and the settings. A stage takes a few minutes; after it, "battle complete", and the next one waits until you start it.

    Fly one stage while the kettle boils, or the whole campaign in an evening.

Game modes

There's one mode, the campaign, and the win log's flashbacks beside it:

From the start screenWhat it's for

Features and mechanics

  1. Simple, not silly

    Real jets, simplified

    The flying follows Fighters!: streamlined and arcade-like, but with a jet's real limits. You can't stop or hover. You can't turn on the spot. You trade speed for a tighter turn, line up on a target from far away, and pull up before the ground does it for you.

    Those limits are the game. A strafing run is a commitment: line up, fire, pass over, loop around. Distances are big, about six minutes across the cube at cruise speed and two on afterburner, so choosing which target to hit next, and from which direction, matters. Flying into terrain is fatal, and ramming an enemy hurts you badly too.

  2. Lock-on

    The camera button and the red box

    Locking on happens on its own: bring your aim point close enough to a target and a missile fired now will track it and home in. Fired without a lock, it flies straight, as a dumb rocket.

    The hardest part of fighting in a jet is knowing where things are, so one button does it for you: hold it and the camera swings to look at the nearest enemy and stays on it until you press again. Together with the red box, the edge arrow and the altitude arrows on the radar, you're never hunting for your next target.

  3. Fallers

    Burning wreckage and chain reactions

    An enemy aircraft that's hit first catches fire and starts to fall, trailing smoke, unable to fly or shoot properly. You can shoot it again on the way down to blow it apart completely, or let gravity finish the job.

    Collisions are real for everyone. An enemy jet chasing you through the hills can be led into one, and a vehicle knocked into the air falls and breaks. Fuel silos and ammunition dumps explode and set their neighbors off, Just Cause and Red Alert 2 style, so the right first shot can take out half a base.

  4. Weapons from shapes

    One stats sheet, many weapons

    Every weapon is a single self-contained object, as in Hotline Miami, and made from one of three kinds of attack: a projectile that flies where it's pointed (no gravity, no arcs), a charged area (a cone, cylinder, line or sphere that visibly charges to 100%, then strikes), or an expanding shape that grows outward and hits on contact. One stats sheet of range, area, fire rate, spread, speed, homing, lock-on time, recoil, push and ammunition turns those into a whole arsenal.

    Aim can be manual, homing with a lock, or smart, steering itself toward the lead point. You carry several weapons and cycle through them with one key. Ammunition is generous, so spraying is fine; only the rare superweapon needs saving. Every shot is one simple thing, with no missiles that split into more missiles.

    Enemies use the same weapons, as in Einhänder, so what they drop is yours: fly close and grab it. Friendly fire is on for everyone, and the enemy hits its own side all the time.

  5. Sea, land and sky

    A world in layers

    Each stage's world is a stack: a flat floor, an optional sea on top of it, and sky up to the ceiling. Big indestructible terrain stands on the floor, as islands where there's sea and as hills, mesas, dunes and mountains where there isn't. Smaller destructible things are scattered on top: seaweed underwater, trees on the ground, clouds in the air.

    The layers can change. A powerful enough superweapon boils away part of the sea, and the water level stays lower for the rest of the stage, uncovering whatever was beneath it.

  6. The regime's army

    Many of them, none of them clever

    The regimes field what real militaries field: tanks, anti-aircraft guns, missile sites, jets, drones, helicopters, gunboats, trucks, artillery, blimps, gunships and bunkers. Every unit is a vehicle with mounts for one to four of the same weapons you use. They have no special abilities, on purpose:

    KindHow it fights
    StationaryShoots far and accurately, but turns slowly to track you
    MobileInaccurate, tries to close in first, turns faster, and sometimes dodges
    Non-combatNever shoots back: parked vehicles, trucks, buildings

    Each unit is worse than your jet in some way: slower to react, unable to lead a shot, prone to hitting its own side. Nobody retreats, repairs or sneaks; there's no stealth and no jamming. They spawn, do their few simple things, and fight to the last.

  7. The finale

    Erasing the name

    The last kill of every stage is a moment. One second after it, time starts to stop: the world slows to a standstill over five seconds, and the controls turn into a free camera so you can look around the wreckage while the credits for that stage scroll past.

    In the tenth stage, the moment the last target goes up, the target's name appears in white, flashes red and fades to black. Then it's deleted, one letter every half second, under the word "erased". No score, no payout: the name is simply gone.

  8. Your jet

    Premade jets, rolled loadouts

    You pick from premade jets, each with its own armor, top speed, acceleration, weapon slots and item slots. Every loadout starts with one reliable weapon and fills the rest at random, so each campaign's first stages feel a little different. A design alternative, after Cryptark, is fixed themed loadouts with their own skins and a reusable special ability. You choose the color of your afterburner flame.

    There's no repair in mid-air. Supply caches wait at the edge of the cube, where you came in: if the ammunition runs low or the fight goes badly, fly back out to the edge, restock, and come back in.

  9. Built from rules

    Stages that generate themselves

    No stage is built by hand. Each is a ten-kilometer cube with a one-kilometer margin around the edge for you and the supply caches, and the eight kilometers in the middle for the enemy:

    1. LayersFloor, an optional sea, and sky up to the ceiling
    2. TerrainIslands, hills or mountains on the floor, kept off the edges
    3. SceneryTrees, seaweed and clouds, sparse enough to fly through fast
    4. TargetsCast a ray down from the ceiling to the highest solid ground, place a unit or a cluster
    5. ClearRemove scenery in each target's radius so nothing spawns inside a tree

    Every campaign is a seed, so a won campaign can be rebuilt exactly for a flashback, and every new kind of unit or terrain piece turns up in all the campaigns that come after it.

Sealed orders

Thunderstrike Justice: Arcade Air Combat

Somewhere tonight, someone with too much power is sleeping well behind their walls, their guns and their army. They're sure nobody can touch them.

Ten strikes stand between you and their front door. Burn the camps, flatten the refineries, knock their jets out of the sky. Then watch the world slow to a stop while their name comes apart, one letter at a time.

Nobody else is coming. You are.
