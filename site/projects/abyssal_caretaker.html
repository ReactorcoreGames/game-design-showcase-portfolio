Abyssal CaretakerOcean Cleanup Ascent

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Abyssal Caretaker is a reverse Subnautica. You start at the bottom of the ocean, five kilometers down, as an ancient frogman whose people have tended the deep for ages, and your journey goes up: through the slope, the shelf and the shallows, toward a port city and the ships that keep dropping things into your home.

It's a side-view survival and crafting game about a lone caretaker with tools and vehicles. You salvage the sunken shipping containers and gather what the sea floor offers, adapt your suit to survive each shallower, brighter, warmer depth, and clean up as you go: seal leaking containers, clear chemical plumes, vacuum up plastics, replant what was crushed, and watch life come back.

It grew out of reading about a container ship that sank off the southwest coast of India in 2025 with hazardous cargo aboard, and wondering what happens to those containers at fifty, five hundred and five thousand meters. The game answers with a janitor who was there long before the ships.

Target audience

Players who loved the solitude, the tools and the vehicles of Subnautica, and the quiet satisfaction of games where you clean, fix and restore rather than fight.

They want to make a broken place whole again, at their own pace, and see it stay that way.

The controls are a familiar side-scroller's: move with the keyboard, aim tools with the mouse, a handful of keys for tools, vehicles and menus.

Game's purpose

Care and contribution: tending a place, with the place itself as the feedback: clearing water, returning fish, regrowing coral.

Discovery and understanding: the ocean from bottom to top, each depth a different world with its own rules.

Creation: turning the wreckage into the tools and suit parts that carry you further.

It avoids draining rest (no timers, no hunting, nothing chasing you) and safety (nothing you've restored is ever undone, and a failed dive costs a nap, not your work).

Core concept

Survival-crafting games usually gate their world behind an upgrade ladder and then run out of reasons to play once the ladder ends.

Here the only gates are the ocean's own physics: pressure, light, heat and air. Each suit adaptation answers one of them.

And the job never ends, because the ships never stop sailing. Once you've reached the port, the whole ocean is your garden, and new wrecks and spills keep arriving for its caretaker.

Lore and story

The caretakers

Long before anyone sailed above them, the abyss had its gardeners: a people of amphibious frogmen, built for crushing pressure, cold and total darkness, who tended the deep the way a forester tends a wood. They cleared what fell, healed what sickened, and left their workbenches and carvings in crypts around the warm hydrothermal vents.

They work slowly and sleep long. Between tasks a caretaker curls up in a vent crypt for an age at a time, and wakes when something needs doing.

What the ships dropped

Up at the surface there are shipping lanes now, and every so often a ship goes down or a storm sweeps its deck. Steel boxes rain into the ocean. Some float for days and sink in the shallows; some reach the slope and crack open; some fall all the way to the abyssal plain and crumple under the pressure. Inside are chemicals, plastics, fuel and things nobody wrote down.

Nobody up there means harm, and nobody up there knows the caretakers exist. The boxes just keep coming.

The waking

You wake in your crypt to find the plain outside littered with crushed containers and the water around them sick. You clean up what's in reach, and find that the trail of wreckage leads upward, toward the light, through depths your body was never made for.

So you go up. Not to change the people at the top, but to look after the water all the way to their harbor.

The suit's state sits in one small gauge in the corner: how well it's coping with the pressure, light, heat and water quality where you are right now. When you swim toward a depth it isn't ready for, the gauge tells you before your body does.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    A work site

    A wreck, a spill, a field of crushed containers. You swim in, scan it, and get to work: cut open what's blocking the way, weld shut what's leaking, spray the plume, vacuum the drifting plastic, pry the salvage loose and haul it out.

    The site answers in real time. The water clears around a sealed container, a calmed creature drifts back to its den, a patch of seabed you've replanted starts to glow.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A cleanup job

    Each incident is one job with a beginning and an end: a sunken freighter's scattered cargo across a slope, a storm's worth of containers on a reef, a fuel sheen under the docks. The job is done when the site's water runs clean and its life has started coming back.

    On the first climb, the jobs lie along your way up, and each depth's job teaches you what that depth is like and yields the materials for the adaptation that carries you to the next.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The whole ocean in your care

    Reaching the port isn't the end. From then on the whole column, abyss to harbor, is yours to tend, and because ships keep sailing above, new incidents keep arriving: generated from what a ship carried, how deep it went down and what lives there.

    Your crypt is the home you return to, with its workbench, stored salvage and parked vehicles. The ocean map is the living record: every site you've cleaned stays clean and keeps recovering, glowing brighter on the map as its life returns. That's what you've done, shown back to you, not a score.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    One dive at a time

    Pick a job on the map, dive to it, work until you're done or until you want to stop. A job takes anywhere from twenty minutes to an evening.

    The game saves at your crypt and at any ancient workbench along the way, and a half-finished site waits exactly as you left it: the leaks you sealed stay sealed. Nothing gets worse while you're away from the game.

Game modes

There's one mode: the ocean. The first climb from the abyss to the port is the first stretch of it, and the tending that follows is the rest. Its five depths, bottom to top, each nest the work inside them:

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Adapting the suit, depth by depth

    Subnautica sends you down into ever-deeper danger. This goes the other way, and the danger is the reverse too. The caretaker is a creature of the abyss: his body expects crushing pressure, cold and darkness, and the shallows would hurt him the way the deep hurts a human.

    So each adaptation answers a real property of the water above. Nothing is locked behind a level or a counter: the ocean itself is the limit, and the suit is how you meet it. Each part is crafted from what you find at or below the depth where you need it.

  2. A janitor's kit

    The caretaker's gauntlet

    Every cleanup tool lives in one gauntlet with swappable modes, plus a few things you place and leave working:

    ToolWhat it does
    Cutting and sealing beamSlices through debris and container walls, and welds leaks and cracks shut
    Bioremediation sprayReleases cleaning bacteria and algae that break down oil and chemicals over time
    Vacuum and filterSucks up microplastics, contaminated silt and small loose debris
    Sampler and scannerIdentifies an unknown chemical, finds plumes, currents, creatures and salvage
    Seed dispenserPlants coral, kelp and seagrass on cleaned ground
    Bubble shieldHolds a spill in place while you deal with its source
    Bio-resonatorCalms a frightened or sickened creature so you can heal it or let it pass

    The order you use them in is the puzzle. A plume sprayed before its container is sealed just keeps coming back; a seabed replanted before the silt is cleaned won't take.

  3. Abundance from the wreckage

    Salvage crafting

    The materials problem in this game is the opposite of most survival games: there's far too much stuff down here, and that's the whole trouble. Every container is a crate of insulation, steel, tubing, filters and odder things, and the sea floor has its own minerals, gels, shells and plants.

    Crafting combines the two, human salvage and the ocean's own materials, at the ancient workbenches in the crypts and outposts. Container insulation and volcanic minerals make a thermal layer; ballast tanks and gas sacs make swim bladders; filter mesh and kelp enzymes make a biofilter. Cleaning up and gathering materials are the same act, so nothing is ever farmed for its own sake.

  4. Feedback you can see

    An ocean that heals

    The point of every job is visible in the water. A cleaned site clears, then keeps recovering on its own: fish return first, then the bigger animals, then the coral and kelp you planted grow in, and bioluminescent life lights up places that were dead zones.

    Come back to an old site later and it's further along. Nothing you restore ever slides back, and new incidents arrive in new places, not as a reset of old ones.

  5. Bigger hands for bigger jobs

    Vehicles and tadpoles

    Three vehicles, each grown from the caretakers' old designs and patched with salvage: the Abyssal Skimmer, a quiet manta-shaped glider for long trips through the deep with cargo pods for big debris and rescued animals; the Midwater Climber, a frog-legged walker with magnetic grips and long arms for cliffs, wrecks and heavy containers; and the Surface Hopper, an amphibious craft for the shallows and the harbor.

    Small tadpole drones help on the bigger sites: scouts that map a wreck and mark hazards, cleaners that collect debris and spread absorbents, and menders that patch containers and tend injured animals while you work on something else.

  6. No weapons

    Gentle hands

    The caretaker doesn't fight. The dangers are the environment's: toxic plumes, collapsing container stacks, hot vents, flammable gas from cargo reacting with seawater, sunlight in the shallows. Creatures driven aggressive by poisoned prey are sick, not enemies, and they're calmed with the bio-resonator and healed, or simply given room.

    Near the surface the caretaker stays unseen, slipping past divers and propellers with the Hopper's cloak, because nobody up there knows his people exist. If a dive goes wrong and the suit gives out, he falls into the caretakers' deep sleep and his tadpoles tow him home. He wakes a little later, everything he cleaned still clean, and goes back to work.

Notes carved on a crypt wall

Abyssal Caretaker: Ocean Cleanup Ascent

Woke to a cold plain full of broken boxes. The water around them tastes wrong. Sealed what I could reach. The trail leads up.

Above the slope the water gets light and warm and thin, and my old shell won't hold. The boxes have what I need; the sea floor has the rest. I'll make it work. Somewhere above is a harbor where the boxes come from, and the water there needs looking after too.

Someone has to tend the deep. It's been us for a long time.
