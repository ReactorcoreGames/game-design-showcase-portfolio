CollidotronCascade Physics Processor

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Collidotron is a calm chain-reaction game. You crack one tile of a glowing slab, and it releases an orb. The orb ricochets through the slab, and every tile it breaks releases an orb of its own. Two become four, four become dozens, until the whole slab has turned into hundreds of orbs bouncing inside the machine.

Then the last tile breaks, and everything is flushed away, sorted and gone. A new slab slides in.

It's loosely inspired by brick breakers, but turned into something to watch and unwind with: one mouse button, no lives, no timer, and a reaction that always finishes. The player is a technician who starts it, and now and then nudges it along.

Target audience

People who unwind by watching something satisfying happen: domino runs, marble machines, a Peggle ball clearing the board, kinetic sculptures, screensavers.

They want to switch off without switching off completely, with a little to do and a lot to look at.

The whole game is one mouse button, with the other for speeding up.

Game's purpose

Rest and peace: a slow, self-running process that asks almost nothing and always resolves.

Beauty: kaleidoscope slabs, multiplying orbs, a gallery that becomes an art collection.

Play and wonder: one touch starting something much bigger than itself.

It deliberately avoids draining calm: there's nothing to lose, no fail state, no score to beat, and the only intervention is optional.

Core concept

A brick breaker keeps you tense: one ball, reflexes, and a life lost every time it gets past.

Here the bricks break each other. Every broken tile adds an orb, orbs can't be lost, and the reaction carries itself to the end.

Your part is one spark and the odd nudge, and the generated slabs and quirks keep every cascade different.

Lore and story

Landfills turned to stone

By the 23rd century, centuries of waste had filled the landfills far beyond capacity. Instead of burying or burning it, scientists seeded the landfills with synthetic microbial and chemical catalysts. The agents bonded with the plastics, metals and organics and sped up geology itself, until the whole mass hardened into crystalline blocks of hybrid matter.

That material is called strata-mass. It isn't mined as ore but cut out in loaves: solid blocks of turned-to-stone landfill, each sliced into thin slabs.

The Collidotron

The Collidotron is a material disassembly reactor. A slab is locked into its titanium frame, and one precise pulse fractures a single tile, freeing a reaction orb: a tiny packet of stored kinetic energy that the machine keeps moving at a constant speed. The orb shatters the tiles it hits, each freeing its own orb, and the cascade runs until the slab has fully collapsed.

The released energy is siphoned into batteries. The fragments are flushed into sorting conduits below, separated into metals, ceramics, polymers and silicates, and pressed into standard material pebbles that feed universal assemblers, the 3D printers that make everything from cutlery to circuit boards.

The job right now

You're the technician at the machine. What used to be a smelly landfill is now a shiny mine, and every slab you process cleans up a little more of the old world and turns it into the new one. Load a slab, start the reaction, and keep it running smoothly.

Controls and interface

The view looks straight down into the machine: a slab of square tiles held in a titanium-white frame, over a faint grid. There's almost no HUD at all. The whole game is played with the mouse.

Start the reaction

Left click
Spark any tile; it breaks and sends its orb off at a random angle

During the cascade

Left click
Slow time to a tenth of normal speed
Click an orb
Select it, then click a direction to send it that way
Click away
Cancel the selection and return to normal speed
Right click
Toggle 1.5× speed, overriding the slow motion while it's on

A redirected orb keeps the standard speed; only its direction changes. Nothing else in the game needs input, so the controls can be learned in the first ten seconds and then mostly forgotten.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    One slab, one cascade

    Pick a tile and spark it. Watch the first orb find its way, the second follow, then a flood. Most of the time you just watch. When a lone orb is stuck circling an empty pocket while a corner of the slab sits untouched, slow time down and send it where it's needed.

    The last tile breaks, the orbs drain away, and the slab is done.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A loaf of nine slabs

    A campaign is one loaf of strata-mass, cut into nine slabs of mixed sizes. Each loaf comes from somewhere, an urban landfill, industrial slag, electronic scrap, a seam of biowaste, and carries two or three quirks from its origin that change how all nine of its slabs behave.

    When the ninth slab is done, the loaf is assembled into a 3×3 collage of its slabs, and archived.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The archive

    Every loaf you've processed stays in the archive, a gallery that keeps growing. There's no end to the landfills, so there's always another loaf.

    Over time the archive becomes a logbook of reclaimed matter, half industrial record and half accidental art collection: hundreds of kaleidoscope slabs, each one something you processed.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A slab at a time

    Open the game and the next slab of the current loaf is waiting in the frame. A slab is a few quiet minutes. Process one between tasks, or a whole loaf with an evening's music on.

    Stop after any slab, and the loaf waits where you left it.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    A cascade that carries itself

    Every orb moves at one constant speed, kept up by the machine. It never slows down and never falls. It bounces off tiles and the frame, and passes straight through other orbs. Every tile it breaks releases another orb, so the reaction grows on its own, faster and denser, until there's nothing left to break.

    That's what makes it relaxing. There's no way to lose an orb and no way for the reaction to fail; it only ever gets busier and closer to done. A full slab can end with hundreds of orbs in flight at once, so the game is built to handle up to 600, with light trails and up to a thousand particles of breaking tiles.

  2. The technician's touch

    One spark and the odd nudge

    The player does two things. The first is the spark: choosing which tile cracks first. A tile in the middle of a dense cluster sets off a quick bloom; one at the edge of a void sends its orb on a long lonely trip first.

    The second is the nudge, and it's optional. Slow time to a tenth, pick out any orb, and point it somewhere better, usually at the last stubborn corner of the slab. And when you'd rather just get on with it, right click runs everything at one and a half times the speed.

  3. Grown, not drawn

    Kaleidoscope slabs

    Every slab is generated: a collection of square tiles in a kaleidoscope or fractal pattern, centered or set off to one side, with pockets of empty space making up anywhere from 1% to half of it. Tiles get two to four vivid colors, beveled edges and a thin dark outline so they pop, over an animated gradient background in one to three softer colors, linear, rectangular, curved or round.

  4. What the slab is made of

    Materials

    Each color on a slab is a material, so every slab holds two to four of them. Materials change how a tile breaks and what kind of orb it releases, each with its benefits and drawbacks. A steel tile might take six hits from an orb of compound but only two from a steel orb, and a steel orb might plow straight through soft compound tiles instead of bouncing, until it meets one that takes more than a hit.

  5. Where the loaf came from

    Quirks

    Each loaf brings two or three quirks from its origin, so a whole campaign has a character of its own:

    QuirkWhat it does
    Volatile compoundsSome tiles burst over a larger radius when they break
    Heavy elementsOrbs move slower but break through several tiles
    Magnetic fieldsOrbs fly on curved paths
    Quantum instabilityTiles now and then phase out of existence and back
    Growth patternsPorous voids, dense crystalline clusters or reinforced ridges in the slabs
    LooksLine or micro-explosion trails, fracture glow colors, a different flush

    Rarely, a loaf turns out to be anomalous: a test batch with stranger quirks, extreme symmetry, unusually porous layouts or unpredictable cascade speeds.

  6. A living catalog

    The gallery

    Every slab is saved as a picture and a seed, and every finished loaf as its 3×3 collage, with its generated name, its quirks and a few facts about how it went: how long the reaction ran, how many collisions it took, how efficiently it collapsed. Those numbers are a record, not a grade; nothing is ranked and nothing is earned.

    Any slab can be replayed from its seed, the same tiles in the same pattern, from the same first spark or a new one. The gallery is the game's memory, and over time it becomes yours: an exhibition of every kaleidoscope you ever took apart.
