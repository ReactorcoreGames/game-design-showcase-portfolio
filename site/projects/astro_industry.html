Astro Industry FreelancerInterstellar Construction Jobs

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Astro Industry Freelancer is a Bob the Builder simulator in space. You're an engineer in a robot body, taking jobs from people and organizations all over a peaceful universe: build this, dig that out, move this enormous thing over there, blow up that rock, fix the broken pump, and do it however you like.

You drive heavy haulers, diggers and crushers, carve tunnels through destructible ground, print equipment, set up small automated supply chains, and fly between worlds to get to the next client. There's no combat focus, no adversaries and no clock. The challenge is the logistics puzzle, the hazards and the distances, and every machine is as simple to use as a toy.

It grew out of playing Astroneer through several times and wanting it never to run out, crossed with the open star maps of Freelancer, the digging of Motherload and the machines of Factorio, Satisfactory and Subnautica.

Target audience

Players who love the tactile machines of Astroneer and the wide-open universe of Freelancer, and who reached the end of both with nothing left to do but sit in a finished base.

They want the fantasy every kid has about construction work: big machines, big holes, and a happy client at the end, in a world where nobody's in danger.

The interactions are Duplo-simple: grab, plug in, drive, dig. The depth is in the problems, not the buttons.

Game's purpose

Power and play: driving machines that move mountains, with physics, particles and sound that make every push feel heavy.

Contribution: colorful characters asking for help, and thanking you for it.

Understanding and discovery: logistics puzzles, and a universe whose mysteries are real places you can bring your tools to.

It avoids draining rest and safety: no enemies, no timers, mistakes are allowed, and the worst that happens is a mess to clean up.

Core concept

Factory and survival games run on an early, mid and end game: scarcity first, then a slog of unlocks, then a sandbox with no goals that grows until the computer lags.

Here there's no early, mid or end game, only game. Everything is available from the start, and an endless job generator keeps giving the structure, goals and people that usually vanish after the credits.

The fun isn't in getting the tools. It's in using them.

Lore and story

You

You're a human mind, digitized and transferable, piloting a robot body the way someone else might drive a truck. You can swap its parts, own several bodies, and climb into any machine or vehicle like a person would.

You don't have to work. You do these jobs because you find them fun and like helping people out. And you're about as immortal as anyone gets: a backup of your mind waits on a secure server, and if a body breaks beyond repair in an accident, the backup is restored with every memory up to that moment.

The universe

A future civilization has spread peacefully across solar systems and galaxies: planets, moons, asteroid fields, stations, oceans and deep underground colonies, linked by trade lanes and jump gates. There's always something being built, dug, moved or mended somewhere.

The clients

Your work comes from the people who live out there: a colony that needs a landing pad, a research team whose drill rig got stuck, a moon mine that wants a tunnel, someone who simply wants a very large rock moved off their lawn. They're colorful, they're specific about what they want, and they're happy when it's done.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    A job site

    Arrive, look at the problem, pick your machines. Dig the trench, haul the module, crush the boulder, weld the pipe, set the charges and stand back. Physics does the rest, sometimes better than planned and sometimes not.

    Weather, distance, terrain and hazards get in the way, and solving them is the game: a bridge for the hauler, a tunnel under the storm, a conveyor instead of forty trips.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A campaign of jobs

    Jobs come in generated campaigns, each with its own narrative arc: a new colony that goes from bare rock to a working settlement, a research expedition chasing an anomaly, a mining company opening up a moon. Each campaign has its own pace, order and twists.

    Take its jobs in whatever order you want. The criteria are loose on purpose: if the colony has its water, it doesn't matter that you got it there with three trucks and a small explosion.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    A freelancer's universe

    The universe is a service you never run out of: there are always more clients, and a heuristics-driven generator keeps building new campaigns from where you are and what the world around you is like. Your home is a base or a giant mothership you carry with you, holding your machines, printers and spare bodies.

    Every finished job goes into a logbook with the client's thank-you note: a record of everything you've built, dug and fixed across the stars, not a score to beat.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A job an evening

    Open the job board, pick something that sounds fun, and head out. A small job is half an hour; a campaign runs over many evenings, one job at a time.

    Save whenever you like and stop whenever you like. There's no clock running and no client who minds waiting.

Game modes

There's one mode: freelancing, in one connected universe. Everything sits in a network of scenes you can move between freely, carrying anything from one to the next:

The universeGalaxies and star systems linked by trade lanes and jump gates.
A worldA planet, moon, asteroid field, station, ocean or underground colony.
A job siteOne client's problem, on the ground, underwater, underground or in orbit.

It's built as a singleplayer game. Cooperative play could come later, with friends taking on jobs together live, or a community contributing to each other's worlds asynchronously, never as a contest.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Jobs that never run out

    The design's biggest piece is an infinite job generator built on simple, smartly structured procedural rules. It looks at a place (its terrain, resources, weather, settlements and what's already there) and composes a job that makes sense for it, with a client, a goal and a few obstacles.

    Kind of jobFor example
    BuildLanding pads, habitats, roads, bridges, pipelines, power networks
    DigTunnels, foundations, a mine shaft down to a rare deposit
    MoveHaul cargo between worlds, shift a superheavy object, relocate an outpost
    Blow upClear a rockfall, demolish a condemned building, crack open a sealed cave
    RepairA stuck drill rig, a broken pump station, a damaged satellite
    Craft and modifyPrint parts, refit a vehicle, upgrade a settlement's machines
    SolveAnything else: an anomaly, a flooded mine, a very confused client

    When it's done, the client thanks you, and that thank-you is the point: feedback that your work made someone's day, never a reward that has to keep growing.

  2. Toys for grown-ups

    Heavy machines, simple controls

    Every machine is built like Astroneer's: chunky modules that plug together, with obvious inputs and outputs, so even a toddler could work out what goes where. The satisfaction comes from what they do to the world: sound effects, particles, a head-up display, and visible physics on every prop and tile they touch.

    MachineWhat it's for
    Haulers and cranesMoving material, modules and superheavy objects
    Diggers and tunnelersCarving terrain, trenches, shafts and caves
    CrushersBreaking boulders, wrecks and anything in the way
    Printers and refineriesTurning raw material into parts and equipment
    SpacecraftGetting there, and carrying the heavy hardware with you
    Charges and demolition gearTaking buildings and rock apart, Red Faction-style

  3. Destructible terrain

    Carve the world

    The ground is made of tiles you can dig, blast and rebuild, with loose physics props on top. As in Motherload and SteamWorld Dig, the deeper you go, the rarer the resources and the harsher the hazards: heat, pressure, gas pockets, water breaking through.

    That makes the terrain itself the toolbox. A job might be easiest solved by digging under a problem, or by bringing the hillside down on purpose.

  4. Mild automation

    A supply chain you can set and forget

    For the bigger jobs you can set up a small chain of machines that runs on its own, Factorio-style but kept modest:

    1. DepositA resource in the ground
    2. GathererAn automatic vehicle digs and hauls it
    3. SortingA station splits it by material
    4. RefineryRaw material becomes advanced material
    5. PrinterOut comes whatever the job needs

    Power networks, trains, shuttles and drones tie it together. It's there to take the tedium out, not to become the game: there's no reason to build a factory bigger than the job in front of you, and the universe never asks you to grow one forever.

  5. Between jobs

    A universe you can actually touch

    In space you fly a ship top-down across star maps built the way Freelancer's were: trade lanes, jump gates, buoys, debris fields, nebulae and asteroid belts, with the freedom to mine, haul cargo and deal with the odd hazard or anomaly on the way.

    And the mysteries out there aren't background pictures. A derelict, a strange signal or an alien structure is a real place you can land at, with your heavy hardware, and do something about.

  6. Make all the mistakes you want

    Bodies, backups and messes

    Your body is modular: swap its parts, keep a few different bodies for different work, and climb into anything with a seat. If one gets crushed under a boulder you dropped yourself, your backup wakes up in a spare body with every memory intact.

    The consequence of a mistake is honest and physical: a mess to clean up, a vehicle to fetch from where it fell, a bridge to rebuild. Nothing is taken away and nobody is punished. Everything the game has is available from the very start, so there's no ladder to fall off either.

Job board, new posting

Astro Industry Freelancer: Interstellar Construction Jobs

Wanted: one engineer, any body type. Must enjoy big machines, big holes and other people's problems. No experience required; mistakes welcome; mess will be tidied by the person who made it.

Pay is a warm thank-you and whatever you dig up on the way. The universe is large and nobody's in a hurry.

Bring your own drill. Or print one.
