AAL Eradication WarsApocalyptic Mecha Warfare

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

AAL Eradication Wars is a side-scrolling mecha shooter in the spirit of Soldat, played alone against bots that fight like people. Two teams of transforming war machines, four to sixteen a side, throw themselves around a battlefield while giant Arms Forts grind forward and two fleets tear each other apart in the sky above.

Each side has a pool of tokens, one spent for every death. Drain the enemy's before they drain yours. Battle by battle, region by region, the war decides whether a nation survives at all.

It moves like Tribes and Apex Legends rather than Soldat's stop-and-hop, transforms like Zeta Gundam's waverider, borrows its Arms Forts from Armored Core, its tokens from King Arthur's Gold and its background battles from Gundam Seed Destiny: Battle Assault. It's a war story about how far hatred goes, told entirely through play.

Target audience

Players who loved the chaos of Soldat, Unreal Tournament and Armored Core, but are done with the strangers: ranked ladders, toxic chat and matches that leave nothing behind.

They want the intensity of a full arena, played on their own terms, inside a war that remembers what they did.

It's demanding, but in layers: WASD, the mouse and a left click are enough to win. Everything else is finesse for those who want it.

Game's purpose

Challenge and mastery: movement, aim, weapons and awareness, each with real depth, against opponents with the same machines as you.

Excitement and awe: taking part in an apocalypse at full scale, again and again, never the same twice.

Power: one skilled pilot really can turn a battle.

It deliberately avoids draining fairness (the bots fail like humans, never with inhuman aim), safety (the nations are fictional and generated) and time (save anywhere, every mode states its length).

Core concept

Arena shooters are built around other humans: every match resets, someone has to lose, and the mood follows.

Here the arena is a war played solo. Every battle feeds a campaign whose outcome is never fixed, and the opponents are bots designed to feel like a lobby of people, from goofballs to aces.

Nothing about you is boosted or nerfed: the same rules hold for everyone. Difficulty lives only in how well the other pilots fly.

Lore and story

A sector without faster-than-light travel

The game is set early in humanity's expansion into space, in the AAL Universe's era of the Eradication Wars. There's no faster-than-light drive, only sublight engines and jumpgates, much like Freelancer's Sirius sector: a cluster of star systems grouped into worlds, each with its own planets, moons and colonies.

Two nations, no difference

Bigotry, hate and anger have boiled over. Two nations have decided to wipe each other out completely: no expense spared, no mercy, no collateral damage too great, and no turning back until one side has ceased to exist.

The bitter joke at the heart of it is that the two are identical: the same population, the same resources, the same technology, the same machines. Each campaign generates them fresh, with their own names, colors and leaders, and you fight for one of them. The game never tells you which side is right, because neither is.

A month in

The war broke out everywhere at once. Its first week was an exchange of superweapons that fell mostly on cities, homeworlds included, so even the safest places already carry scars. The next three weeks were spent hunting those superweapons down.

What's left is the conventional military at full strength, and its best weapons: Type-3 Mecha, versatile, armored and easy to build, able to fight on land, at sea, in the air, in space, underwater and underground; and Arms Forts, titanic weapons platforms that can take on each other and a swarm of mecha at once. Both nations use both, because nothing else comes close. The campaign begins a month into what will be a one-year war.

The goal

Keep your nation alive. Win the battles that decide each region, and with enough regions, the war. At the end, one nation is either wiped out or sent fleeing the galaxy on a sleeper ship. Which one depends on you.

Controls and interface

You fly one Type-3 Mecha in side view. The mouse aims, the keyboard moves, and every fight is a constant choice between the two: hurl yourself out of an opponent's gun arc, then spin around and hit them with a salvo.

There's no minimap. Instead, a radial radar around your mecha points toward every enemy in line of sight, every ally and every Arms Fort, with marker size hinting at distance. Anyone aiming at you, ally or enemy, draws a dotted three-dot reticle, five dots for a sniper, so lethal fire from off screen never comes without warning.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    One battle

    A handmade map, from a narrow 4-versus-4 corridor to an open 16-versus-16 battlefield with low, middle and high routes between the two sides. Each team has a token pool: about a hundred on small maps, up to a thousand in the epic ones.

    The goal is simple and never changes: kill, and don't get killed. There are no flags, nodes or cores to think about. Everything else on the map, from Arms Forts to falling debris, is there to spice up the fight and can be ignored. A battle has its lulls too: the trip back to the front, terrain that breaks line of sight, the seconds after a respawn.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A region

    A region is fought over in five major battles: two on each nation's ground and one in no man's land. Civilians of whichever nation owns the map shelter inside its buildings, and every kill on the battlefield, civilian or pilot, takes a little off that nation's population.

    Whoever wins the region pushes the loser back to a sixth map, the last stand in front of its megashelters. Hold it, and most of those people evacuate. Lose it, and they don't. Within a region the pilots fight harder as it nears its end, because the stakes are higher; then the next region begins calmer again.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The war

    The war is five regions: two belonging to each nation and one contested. Whoever takes the most invades the other's home: its outskirts, then a space colony, a moon and a planetary city, and finally the last stronghold, where a giant sleeper ship waits to carry the survivors out of the galaxy. Every token left over from earlier battles joins that final fight, on whichever side kept it.

    If the invaders win, the nation is gone. If the defenders hold, they escape into exile, and the epilogue says honestly how their chances look, from a thriving new home to a slow fall back toward the stone age. The war's record goes into a log of past campaigns: the nations' names and colors, the outcome, the final count. The next war is between two new nations.

    Each campaign also ends with a row of medals for what you did (most kills, fewest deaths, the longest shot, times cooked by a nuclear blast) and a ridiculous generated title that grows longer with every medal, as comic relief after a very dark year. A personal hall of records keeps your best moments across every war.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    From five minutes to a whole war

    The mode screen shows each mode's typical length, from five minutes to a war spread over many evenings. Setting one up takes seconds: name the nations (or let the game do it), pick their colors, press play. A short briefing, and you're in.

    The pause menu works anywhere, and you can save and quit in the middle of a battle. Every intermission between battles is a natural place to stop. A clip key saves the last ten seconds of play, so the moment worth showing someone is never lost.

Game modes

The campaign is the main way to play. The others are shorter, and most of them take place in the war's afterlife.

ModeWhat it isLength
CampaignThe whole war: five regions of six battles, then the five-map invasionMany evenings
One regionFive battles and the last standOne to two hours
Quick battleOne random map (or one you pick), then the statsAbout fifteen minutes
Despair MatchPilots who wandered off the front fight on as restless ghosts: a short set of deathmatches back to back, solo or in teams. Master the set and the ghost finally finds peace.Ten to thirty minutes
God ModeOne pilot holds an artifact that makes them a god, on a chaotic map where deranged Arms Forts shoot at the sky. Only kills made as the god count.One map
ClimbingA generated gauntlet of hazards with no combat, or a single duel, ending at a hideout to blow upFive to fifteen minutes

Any mode can be seasoned with a few toggles: atomic nightmare (superweapons only, raining down everywhere, like the war's first week), a longer or shorter campaign (three to eleven regions or battles), the bots' skill, and a seed for the map shuffle. Maps are always shuffled, except in a quick battle, where you can choose one.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Momentum, not friction

    Soldat had wonderful moves, but it braked you to a stop the moment you let go of a key, so even running forward was a chore of constant hopping. Here a Type-3 Mecha is a heavy machine that keeps its momentum: it slides, skis and flows, and takes a counter-boost to slow down. Brakes only kick in once you're nearly stopped.

    On top of that come double jumps timed for height, wall jumps and wall kicks, a continuous floor slide, a drop kick from the air, hovering like a turret, and a boost dash aimed with the mouse and charged by holding, up to an Armored Core-style overboost for crossing the map. There's no fall damage at all: mecha can drop from any height and slam into any wall, and the impact sends out a shockwave that wrecks props nearby.

    Press X and the mecha folds into a waverider, as in Zeta Gundam: a fast flying form steered with the mouse, harder to hit, with its gun limited to a narrow cone ahead. Boost and flight each draw on their own stamina.

  2. Every death counts

    Tokens and the entry

    Every mecha on the field is your equal, so you'll die often. A death takes three to five seconds and costs one of your team's tokens, your kill streak, everything you picked up, and the trip back to the front. The game keeps count of your deaths, too, purely so you know. Once in a while, dying is the fast way home: a self-destruct gets you back to a base under attack quicker than flying there, for the price of a token.

    Mecha come back in style. A vanguard booster hurls you onto the map from off screen with a jet's roar; a second later it falls away, the mecha inside starts up with a turbine whine, and you take control. For one more second you're invulnerable while the entry plates shed, then the mecha flashes from grey into its colors, and everyone knows it can be hit. A small red arrow warns of every incoming mecha two seconds before it arrives.

    Health repairs itself, but very slowly: a full bar takes over three minutes, while most kills take seconds. Med kits and the base are the real way back.

  3. Titans on rails

    Arms Forts

    Both sides field Arms Forts: giant NPC weapons platforms on land, at sea, in the air, in space, underwater and underground, from Armored Core-style fortresses to naval battleships and carriers. Each has its own token pool, separate from the mecha's, and moves along a rail through the corridors big enough for it.

    An Arms Fort goes after structures first: the enemy's spawn shields, linear catapults, stationary guns and supply systems. Once its whole rail is clear, it turns hunter, patrolling to the farthest point an enemy has reached and lying in ambush there. Its turrets, cores and engines can be shot, kicked and destroyed, and it dies in a huge fireball and shockwave.

    A friendly Arms Fort is also a moving supply depot: dock with it for ammunition and repairs, or grab the kits it drops nearby. Some tunnels and rooms are too tight for any Arms Fort, and there it's mecha against mecha only.

  4. The sky is the scoreboard

    The battle in the background

    Above every map, two fleets duel. Each side's ten ships stand for its token pool, and for every tenth of a team's tokens lost, one of its ships is hit and explodes, in no set order. A glance at the sky tells you how the battle is going.

    That war spills into yours. Stray shells and missiles, supply pods and powerups, falling debris, ejection pods and wreckage rain onto the field, along with weather and disasters: storms of lightning, gamma bursts, a falling fuel silo, a colony laser cutting across the map. The danger follows a curve, about 1% near the spawns and 75% in the middle of no man's land, which is also where most of the best supplies land.

    Background walls shelter you from some of it (a gamma burst, but not a meteor), and small bunkers protect against almost everything, though they're easy to storm.

  5. No ceiling on the arsenal

    Weapons

    Rifles, bazookas, launchers and cannons of every kind, as in Gundam, Armored Core and MechWarrior: kinetic and energy, fire, acid, frost, electric and gravity, all the way up to outright superweapons like nuke launchers and ion cannons. There's no limit to how many can exist. Each battlefield deals both sides the same random list of ten primaries and five secondaries, and rare exotics from outside the list arrive as drops.

    KindHow it works
    PrimariesFull-power weapons for sustained fighting, chosen at spawn
    SecondariesSpecialized, light or unconventional; drop yours to carry a second primary instead
    GrenadesTwo or three to start with; other types are found on the field
    PowerupsTemporary: overshield, cloak, berserk, speed, rate of fire, immortality and many more, all wearing off in time
    ExoticsSuperweapons delivered as drops; a few ignore friendly fire

    What keeps a nuke launcher fair next to a machine gun is its costs, as in Cryptark: weight that slows you or locks out a move, slow turning while firing, recoil, reload and draw times, charge-ups, slow projectiles, limited shots, or a weapon that destroys itself after a few uses. Most standard weapons are snappy, with reserves that last tens of reloads, and kill fast.

  6. Everything is physical

    Weapons in hand

    Guns are objects, not menu entries. Drop yours, pick up a fallen friend's or enemy's, or hurl it: a heavy gun thrown at speed is a weapon in itself. With empty hands, a wire reels in the nearest loose gun, even one in mid-flight, and some of its momentum carries you with it. Switching weapons takes a moment that grows with the weapon's heft, and when you're out of everything there are always fists.

    You can't hurt your own side, so you never hesitate, but your shots still push allies, civilians and loose props, which can launch a friend or bat a civilian out of harm's way. You can hurt yourself, at half strength: rocket jumps are real, and so is running into your own grenade.

  7. Difficulty without cheating

    Pilots, not numbers

    Everything in the game is standardized, so health, damage and speed are never adjusted for difficulty, for you or anyone else. Changing them would make the world feel fake. Difficulty lives entirely in the bots, and the bots are designed to fail like humans: late to notice a shell, panicking into the blast, missing moving targets, forgetting to jump, grenading their own feet, camping, or bolting after two teammates die beside them.

    Each battle rolls a roster of pilot profiles, each with a callsign on a live scoreboard. Most are average, and each difficulty setting changes the mix:

    SettingTotal novicesAverageVeteransElites
    Lowest15%65%15%5%
    Highest5%30%50%15%

    Even an elite slips up now and then. You keep the edges a human has: faster reactions, bots that shoot where you are instead of where you'll be, and weapon combinations and hazards they never think of. They're dumb, but they're holding a dangerous gun.

  8. Voices of war

    Quips, reports and propaganda

    The pilots are people, and they talk. Short quips pop up above their mecha when they spot an enemy, reload, grab a heavy gun, admire a friendly Arms Fort or die. The chat log carries more, and what you hear depends on where you are: on your own side, honest reports from your team and enemy propaganda; deep on theirs, the reverse; in no man's land, nothing but propaganda from both.

    The propaganda is written the way propaganda really works: manipulation, gaslighting and logical fallacies, the language of contempt. Between battles the enemy sends a longer message, gloating if they won and whining if they lost, and your teammates can be asked what trick it's trying to pull. Your own side never talks to each other that way. The ugliness exists only between enemies, aimed at the other side as a weapon.

    Chat can be muted down to priority warnings, and quips reduced to emoji.

  9. The cost, shown plainly

    Billions, counting down

    Both nations' populations, in billions, sit quietly at the edge of the screen, with the local count beneath them. They dim and drop with every kill. At the start of a battle, civilians are still running for their shelters; some don't make it.

    When a region's last stand falls, its megashelters are left open, and the game shows what happens next in full: the screaming, and then a silence that doesn't lift. It's never a prize. Nothing counts it, scores it or hands out a medal for it, and you don't have to take part. It's there because this is what a war of eradication is, and because it can happen to your side's people just as easily.

Mobilization order

AAL Eradication Wars: Apocalyptic Mecha Warfare

Report to your Type-3 Mecha. The superweapons are spent and the cities are burning. Two fleets are already firing over the next battlefield, and your nation's token count is on the board.

Fly fast. Keep moving. Pick up what the fallen drop. Watch the sky: every ship that goes down is another tenth of someone's tokens gone.

One year. One war. One nation left.
