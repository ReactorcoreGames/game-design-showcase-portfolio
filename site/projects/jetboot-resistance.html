Jetboot ResistanceRagdoll Liberation Raids

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Jetboot Resistance is a ragdoll action game about floppy, wobbly freedom fighters in jet boots taking a country back from its occupiers, one raid at a time. Drop into a region, blow up what holds it down in any order and any way you like, and get out when you're done.

The bodies come from Totally Accurate Battlegrounds: soft, rubbery, physics-driven characters who flail, tumble and climb. The pace sits between Just Cause 2's sandbox demolition and Soldat's jet-boot acrobatics, slowed down whenever you want by bullet time and a full tactical freeze. The structure is the opposite of the battle royale that inspired it: no shrinking circle, no ninety-nine other players who must lose, just a world of occupied regions and a resistance that answers their calls.

Also inspired by Red Faction Guerrilla, Fallout 3, Human Fall Flat, Warning Forever, Duality, Eastward, Gundam Seed, Star Fox 2, Rogue Legacy and Streets of Rogue.

Target audience

Players who laugh out loud at physics slapstick and love taking a place apart their own way: Just Cause and Red Faction demolition fans, TABG fans who wanted more than one match, Soldat players who miss jet boots.

It's keyboard and mouse, Halo-and-Soldat style. Precision is never a gate: bullet time and the tactical freeze stop the world whenever you need to think or aim.

Game's purpose

Play and silliness: wobbly bodies, flying soldiers, chain-reaction explosions.

Freedom and power: any target, any order, any route, any tool, and the sky is yours in jet boots.

Meaning: regions that stay free, people who write back, and a resistance whose fallen are remembered.

It deliberately avoids draining rest (time stops on demand, and you choose when to extract) and respect for your effort (nothing you destroy ever un-happens).

Core concept

A battle royale makes everyone but one player lose and throws the match away. Open-world liberation games end when the map is cleared.

Here the world is the opponent, and it gets weaker as you win: every boss you remove leaves his goons clumsier and more desperate. A region is a finite campaign with its own shape, and there's always another region calling for help.

Lore and story

The country

A wide, ordinary land of farms, rivers, forests, mountains and small towns, carved into regions a few kilometres across. Some are green and wet, some dry and dusty, some poisoned by industry. Each one is somebody's home.

The occupiers

Region by region, the land has been taken over by occupiers: crime syndicates, private armies and juntas, each one generated with its own name, bosses and habits, and each defined by what it does, from greed to cruelty to plain laziness. They squeeze their regions through bases, depots, propaganda and checkpoints, and they have a weapon nobody else has.

That weapon is the murk: a sickly green fog pumped out by machines, which wipes an area clean of life and keeps the rest of the region afraid. It drifts with the wind and the weather. Some occupiers use the hush instead, a radiation emitter that the weather can't move, with weaknesses of its own. The secret is what the murk does to objects left inside it: it enhances them. Wiping out a valley is how the occupiers farm it.

The resistance, and the job right now

You lead the resistance from a hideout in the hills. It isn't one hero: it's a group of volunteers in red scarves and jet boots, and whoever is up next takes the gun. Occupied towns radio for help, and you answer: send a squad, break the occupier's grip on the region, and bring people home. When a region is free, its people send word, sometimes new volunteers, and the next call has already come in.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    Wobble, fly, break things

    A floppy body that can grab anything, climb anything and wear anything, jet boots under it, and a world full of things that explode. You vault a wall, ride a jet up to a guard tower's roof, drop through it, and knock the guard out of his own window.

    The pace is deliberately slower than a twitch shooter, closer to Just Cause 2, and you can slow it further at any moment. Physics does the comedy: soldiers flail, props topple into each other, a fuel barrel turns one mistake into a chain reaction.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A region campaign, raid by raid

    A campaign is one occupier's hold on one region. Its map shows undiscovered sites, as in Fallout 3, and you choose what to do next: a Base Breaker raid on a part of the region, or a Murk Delve into a fogged-in zone. Each raid is a session in itself, with a drop-in, a list of targets and an extraction.

    You deploy a random squad of five resistance fighters for the whole campaign, and they are its lives. The campaign ends when the occupier's last boss falls and his grip is broken. Its map fills with colour as sites fall, and when it's done you name the freed region and write one line about it, saved as an image with the map.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The resistance answers calls

    The overarching goal is a service that never runs out: occupied towns radio for help, and the resistance goes. Freed regions write back, with thanks, news and new volunteers, and new occupiers keep moving in from the edges of the world map, so there's always another call. They never arrive stronger than the last ones: each is just another regime with its own habits.

    The hideout is the persistent home: the armory where everything brought back is kept, the radio room, workshops and people sent by freed towns, and the memorial wall. The world map remembers every region you've freed, with its name and line. All of it records what happened, and none of it is a score.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    One raid, or a whole region

    Start in the hideout, answer a call or continue a region, pick a site on the region map, and drop in. A raid takes roughly half an hour to an hour, and you choose where and when to extract. A region campaign is a handful of raids or a long string of them, depending on its rolled length.

    The game saves between raids and on quit. Stop after any raid and the region waits exactly as you left it.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Floppy bodies in jet boots

    Every character is a physics-driven ragdoll that stands, runs and balances by itself, wobbling the whole time, like TABG's fighters. As in Human Fall Flat, a fighter can grab and climb ledges, pull levers, and pilot any machine or vehicle they can get into.

    Jet boots are standard equipment: higher jumps, short flights, superman dives, backflips, rolls, dropkicks and a long knee slide across level ground. Movement has real momentum, as in classic Doom II, so a slope speeds you up and a jet-boost carries you on. All of it is played for the slapstick: enemies are just as floppy as you are, and a dropkick from a jet-boosted jump sends a guard cartwheeling.

  2. Thinking over reflexes

    Bullet time and the tactical freeze

    Bullet time slows everything on demand. The tactical freeze stops time completely: you aim freely while it's stopped, every shot lands where you aimed, and there's no limit on using it. It's Fallout's targeting mode without the dice.

    While frozen, the scene is colour-coded: red for enemies, blue for allies and neutrals, green for your squad, purple for the fallen, and orange for anything worth shooting, like a fuel tank or a crane's cable. It turns a chaotic yard into a plan.

  3. Carry anything, feel everything

    Weight, kits and the body you have

    Fighters start as a body with jet boots: what they become depends on what they pick up. Suits, exoskeletons, backpacks and armor sets turn a fighter into a class while worn, as in Planetside or Earth Defense Force, and drop off again when removed. Each fighter also has four rolled stats (health, speed, melee and ranged) and a few perks, in the manner of Streets of Rogue.

    Every item has a weight, and a load changes the body, not just a number:

    Carrying heavyWhat changes
    WorseShorter jumps and jet-boot flights, slower grapple pulls, turns, walking, running, rolls and ledge pulls, shorter slides (especially uphill), backflips nearly useless, faster falls
    BetterFaster slides downhill, less recoil, harder to knock over, and a much bigger body slam

    So the load is a choice: travel light and fly, or go heavy and become a battering ram.

  4. Content that multiplies

    Sites built from a budget

    Every site is assembled when its raid begins. A heuristic tree decides what kind of place it is (a fuel depot, a factory, a barracks, a comm hub), gives it a budget, spends the budget on fitting props and places them first-come-first-served, then puts guards in the spaces left over. Each new prop added to the pool turns up in every kind of site that fits it.

    The props are Just Cause and Cryptark material: statues, comm towers, fuel silos and barrels, ammunition depots, propaganda billboards and trucks, generators, transformers and servers, SAM sites, artillery, turrets, jammers, shield generators, assemblers and refineries, alarm systems, and the controllers for buzzsaws, crushers and fire jets. A hotter-colder detector points out the targets you still have to wipe out at each site, so a messy fight never ends in a search for the last satellite dish.

  5. The enemy weakens as you win

    Bosses and their edge

    Each occupier has a handful of bosses scattered through the region, and you don't know where until you find a clue or stumble into one. Every boss gives the occupier an edge: a bonus that bolsters its grip on the region and powers its goons, like faster patrols, better gear or sharper aim. It's a version of the supercore, a mechanic that runs through Reactorcore's designs.

    Take a boss out and his edge doesn't just vanish: it turns into a chaos trait for everyone who relied on it. The goons become reckless, fearful, panicked, confused, slow or apathetic, and some simply desert. The less of the region an occupier controls, the more desperate and clumsier its people get. That's the opposite of a scaling enemy: winning makes the world easier, as it would in real life.

  6. The second kind of raid

    The murk

    A Murk Delve zone is sealed by fog pumped out by murk machines dotted around its edge. The machines define the fog's boundary: destroy the ones on one side and the fog shrinks to the combined reach of the ones still running. It's the battle royale's closing circle turned around: you're the one closing it, on whichever side you choose.

    The murk drifts with the wind and the weather, so its edge moves. The occupiers can also fire it in short area-denial blasts or leave lingering hotspots that make a whole stretch impassable unless you're willing to pay something to cross. Inside it, gear and artifacts left in the fog have been enhanced, and those finds come home to the armory, where they're used until they wear out. Sometimes the zone's heart is guarded by a giant machine assembled from a random tree of modules, each with a vulnerable core, as in Warning Forever.

  7. The enemy, in their own words

    Tapes

    Guards record things, and the tapes lie around their bases. They can point to a boss's whereabouts or to a cache of special gear, give away a site's weak point ("we stored way too many fuel barrels here"), leak classified news (an experimental tank at the next base), or just let you hear the crew: how afraid of you they are, what they think, the propaganda they repeat.

    Their thoughts are usually ugly, sometimes mixed with something human. The tapes don't excuse anyone; they show that the people holding the region are people, which makes the world feel lived in. Every tape you find goes into the hideout's archive.

  8. Forces, not handmade maps

    Generated regions with their own shape

    A region is a square of 6×6 to 12×12 kilometres, generated by a tree of if/else rules from a few parameters (height, temperature, humidity, pollution), so forests, plains, lakes, mountains and deserts appear where they'd naturally be, with a small chance of the unexpected. Neighbouring regions shape each other's borders, as the map in Star Fox 2 does. Now and then a call comes from orbit, and the region is a zero-gravity debris field of rock, ice, gas and scrap, with radiation where a sun is close.

    Each campaign also rolls its own parameters: how long it is, its difficulty shape (it might open hard, ease off and end hard, or stay calm throughout), the weather, how well supplied the occupier is, and whether a giant guardian waits in its murk. Every region comes from a seed, so a good one can be shared.

  9. Someone always takes up the gun

    A squad of five, and the memorial wall

    There's no single protagonist. Each region campaign deploys a random squad of five from the resistance, each fighter with a name, colours, stats and perks of their own, and they are that campaign's lives. When one goes down, the next launches in from any point on the zone's edge, freely chosen: straight back to where the last one fell, or somewhere else first.

    Nothing un-happens. Every target destroyed stays destroyed, every boss stays gone, and the gear a fallen fighter carried lies where they fell for the next one to recover. If all five fall, the campaign stops there, and the region waits as it was left until a new squad answers its call.

    The fallen go on the memorial wall in the hideout, with their portrait, colours, how they fell and what they did. Their names are never used again, as in Rogue Legacy's line of heirs.

The people in the way

Occupied regions still have people living in them, and some are held by the occupiers. You can free them, and freed towns are the ones who write back. In a physics sandbox, keeping them out of the blast is part of the craft. The game never pays out for hurting them, and what happens to them is part of the region's record either way.

The occupiers are never a real nation, party or group. They're generated regimes built from behaviour that anyone could fall into, which keeps the comedy on the physics and the villains on their deeds.

Trivia

The design began as a singleplayer battle royale ("be the last weirdo standing") and turned into its opposite. The Base Breaker raid kept the battle royale's big drop-in map and lost everything else, and the Murk Delve kept the closing circle but handed you the controls.

Radio call from an occupied valley

Jetboot Resistance: Ragdoll Liberation Raids

To whoever is listening in the hills: they've put up a tower on the ridge, a depot by the river, and a fog in the north valley that nobody walks out of. We count six bosses. We don't know where they sleep.

Bring boots. Bring something that goes bang. Bring friends: if one of you falls, the next one comes in from wherever you like. We'll keep the kettle on.

Lace up. Light up. Take the valley back.
