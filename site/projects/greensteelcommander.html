Green Steel CommanderTurn-Based RTS Hybrid

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Free to forkNo licence and no copyright claim: build this game under your own name, or lift one idea from it. Why?

Game overview

Green Steel Commander looks and feels like a Command & Conquer game, Red Alert 2 or Tiberian Sun, and plays like Advance Wars 2: turn by turn, at your own pace. Units roll smoothly across the terrain instead of hopping between squares, and you command them with every convenience a real-time strategy game has ever had, but the clock only moves when you tell it to.

It's a spinoff of Admiral Wars Beyond, set in the same universe with the same persistent campaign, and it aims for a mix of comfy and exciting: Nintendo's cartoony take on war, with the slow, relaxed calm of Tiberian Sun, and opponents who are never scarier than you.

The point is to take the stress out of real-time control and the busywork out of both genres, so that what's left is the part worth thinking about: what to use, how much, where and when.

Target audience

Players who love the look of a classic RTS, its bases, tank columns and command tools, but not its frantic clicking, and players who love Advance Wars' calm turns but felt boxed in by its grid.

They want to plan like a commander without racing anyone's actions per minute.

It's mouse and hotkeys, with automation doing the chores, and nothing ever happens while you're still thinking.

Game's purpose

Understanding and creation: battle plans, base layouts and logistics you cultivate, keep and reuse.

Power and play: a whole army moving at your word, with tanks that shove, flip and knock each other about.

Meaning: battles that count in a universe that remembers them.

It avoids draining rest (no real-time pressure) and ease (no micromanagement, no cheating enemies).

Core concept

RTS games reward speed of hands more than quality of plans; grid tactics games give time to think but turn every battle into a puzzle with one right answer.

Here time stops while you plan, units move and collide freely when it runs, and attacks land exactly as the numbers say.

Every battle sits in a persistent universe that keeps going, so nothing resets after the victory screen.

Lore and story

The same universe, closer to the ground

Green Steel Commander shares its universe with Admiral Wars Beyond: a grounded near future where human powers, rival factions and stranger things fight over worlds, from underground tunnels to orbit. Where that game has you as an admiral moving pieces on a grid, this one puts you in charge of a battlefield the way a classic RTS does, with bases, factories and columns of armor.

You

You're a field commander. You choose the missions, plan the bases, pick the army and give the orders; the logistics, the harvesting and the brick-laying are handled by the people under you. It's a war seen through a cartoony, almost cozy lens, the way Advance Wars sees it: serious about tactics, never grim about it.

The other side

There's no unbeatable mastermind waiting at the end. The opponents are commanders and their mooks, equal to you or weaker, and they never cheat: no extra money, no seeing through the fog. Some of them come back again and again, as rivals, as uneasy neutrals or as allies, and remember how the last meeting went.

The job right now

Take the next mission the universe offers, win it your way, and come home to the base you've been building.

While it's your turn, the battlefield is frozen. You can select, order, queue, rearrange and change your mind as long as you like. When you end the turn, everything moves for a short stretch, smoothly, like a real-time battle, then freezes again for the next set of orders.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    Plan, then press play

    Each turn starts with time stopped. You look over the field, send a tank group down a chain of waypoints, tell the artillery to hold, paint a new wall and bump a factory's order to the front of the queue.

    Then you press play. Everything moves at once, smoothly and physically: columns roll out, an explosion shoves a jeep sideways, a heavy tank nudges a light one out of its way. Attacks land with guaranteed damage, and the turn stops again with the field rearranged and your next decision waiting.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A mission

    A mission is one battle with its own map, goal and conditions. How your army is supplied depends on the kind of mission:

    Mission kindHow the army works
    Standing rosterYou choose the force you want to keep in the field, and production builds and replaces it automatically
    Limited resourcesAn XCOM-style squad you look after, with factories like Advance Wars 2's to build reinforcements from what you have

    Either way, the choice is yours to make once: what to bring and in what mix. The game handles the rest.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The persistent universe

    Around the battles runs the same metagame as Admiral Wars Beyond: a persistent universe that generates missions from what's happened, with events that play out locally, regionally and everywhere at once.

    Its people persist, in a nemesis-like system of rivals, neutrals and allies who remember you. Its places persist too: your home, the sites you fought over and the ruins of enemy bases can all be visited again, and a base you laid out with care is still there when you come back to it.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A battle, or an evening of them

    Open the universe map, choose a mission, and play it at whatever speed you think. A battle is the whole commitment, and because it's turn-based, it saves every turn and waits mid-battle for as long as you're away.

    Between missions, tinker with your army, your saved base layouts and your battle plans, or skip straight to the next fight.

Game modes

There's one mode: the universe, as in Admiral Wars Beyond. Every battle sits somewhere inside it:

The universePersistent and endless: factions, events, people and places that remember.
A missionOne battle on one map, on one to four layers: an evening or less.
A turnOrders with time stopped, then a short stretch of motion.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    Turns without a grid

    Advance Wars' calm comes from its turns, and its stiffness comes from its grid. This keeps the turns and drops the grid. Units move smoothly across the terrain, and each one is a physical object with its own collision box: it blocks a road, can be pushed, can be shoved aside by something heavier, and might even be flipped.

    That brings physics into play without the twitch: explosions knock things back, guns recoil, units pull, push and shove, and heavy machines have weight. A narrow pass blocked by one stubborn tank is a real problem, and a well-placed shell that knocks it into the ditch is a real solution.

  2. No micromanagement

    Every RTS convenience, none of the rush

    Grid tactics make you move every unit by hand; RTS games make you do it fast. Here you get the RTS toolbox and all the time in the world: box, type, screen and all-unit selection with add and remove modifiers, control groups, chained waypoints, rally points, formations and lines, guard, scatter and deploy, and behavior modes for whether a unit should move, shoot or get on with its job.

    An order given once keeps working. A tank group told to hold a ridge holds it turn after turn until you say otherwise, so a big battle never turns into fifty separate clicks a turn.

  3. Bases you paint

    Ghosts, girders and build queues

    There's no build grid; collision boxes keep things from overlapping. Walls and structures go down with a placement tool like Worms' girders: drag them out at any angle, and they appear as a green ghost of where they'll stand.

    A constructor then materializes the ghost gradually. It's solid from the first moment and has as much health as has been built so far, so a half-finished wall already stops something. You paint the layout and the order of construction, and the base builds itself; build queues can be reprioritized ("this first, then carry on as planned"), and you can step in by hand whenever you want. Resources are gathered automatically.

  4. No dice

    Guaranteed attacks and fuel

    Combat works the way Advance Wars' does, not the way a simulation does. There are no physical projectiles flying around to miss: an attack is resolved by the numbers, with no random rolls, so you always know what an attack will do before you order it.

    Fuel works as it does in Advance Wars too, so supply lines and the trucks that carry them matter, and a fast push that outruns its fuel is a real risk you can see coming. Logistics stays simple, in the spirit of Line Wars: what to supply and where, never how many crates go on which truck.

  5. No "Simon says"

    Your army, your way

    Fixed factions, fixed tech trees and missions with one intended solution make a strategy game a game of following instructions. The design offers two ways around it, and they can work together:

    ApproachHow it works
    A generic rosterOne endlessly expandable roster of Advance Wars 2-style units, shared by everyone; what makes a faction different is its commander's passive abilities
    A builderAn Armored Core-style unit and commander builder: design them, then commit to them for the next battle or several

    The builder can double as a workshop, like the weapon builder in Worms World Party, where players make and share units built into the game. Base layouts and battle plans are kept too, so what you've worked out once becomes something you cultivate and reuse, not something thrown away after the battle.

  6. Ground you can change

    Layers, zones and malleable terrain

    Battles keep Admiral Wars Beyond's stacked layers: up to four on a planet (underground, surface, air and orbit), or two in space (inner and outer). Terrain comes in circular and square zones rather than tiles, a mix of Freelancer's space fields and Advance Wars' forests and mountains: drive into a forest zone and you're in the forest.

    And the ground isn't fixed. Terrain can be dug, blasted and built on, so a ridge can lose its top, a river crossing can be made where there wasn't one, and a fortified line can be undone from underneath.

Commander, the console is yours. The base is laid out the way you left it, the factories are humming, and the tank groups are waiting on the ridge.
