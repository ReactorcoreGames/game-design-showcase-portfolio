Zero Space Robo DerbyFirework Arena Shooter

Reactorcore's Personal Game Design Project

Game overview

Zero Space Robo Derby is a small, laid-back arena shooter about a hobbyists' meet in space. A handful of home-painted robot ships drift around a walled arena in zero gravity, charging up slow blaster shots, and every ship that's knocked out bursts into a shower of falling-sand pixels, a little firework that's different every time.

It's flown with the mouse alone: aim to turn, hold one button to thrust, hold the other to charge. You drift with your momentum like in Asteroids, hide behind rocks that split when they're shot, and grab power-ups that bounce around the arena.

At the end of the meet, whoever scored most gets the honor of pressing the button on the big one: a giant zero-gravity bonfire of pixels that every contestant gets to watch. It's a demolition derby in the spirit of Robot Wars, with the crashes replaced by charged shots and the wreckage replaced by fireworks.

Target audience

Players who love the drift of Asteroids, the pop of a good particle effect, and a short arcade round that's easy to pick up and slow to master.

They want a few minutes of play and a pretty show, not a ranked ladder, a loadout grind or a match against strangers.

The interface is the lightest possible: one hand on the mouse, three inputs, and nothing else to learn.

Game's purpose

Play and beauty: every knockout is a cosmetic piñata, a cellular-automata burst you never see twice.

Excitement and mastery: leading a drifting target with a shot that takes seconds to charge is easy to try and hard to perfect.

Celebration and belonging: it's a community party, and the winner's prize is a show for everyone.

It avoids draining rest (rounds are short and the meet waits between stages) and respect (every ship has the same hardware, so nothing is bought or ground for).

Core concept

Arena shooters usually pit you against other people for a rank, and hand out better weapons the longer you play.

Here the other contestants are computer-controlled hobbyists, every ship is the same machine under its paint, and the power-ups are shared by whoever touches them first.

Winning doesn't climb anything. It decides who launches the bonfire, and the bonfire goes into an album along with every other meet's.

Lore and story

The meet

Out on a frontier station, a community of space colonists gets together every so often to crash robot ships for the fun of everyone watching. It's a hobby event, not a sport: nobody's career depends on it, the commentators know half the pilots by name, and the snacks are as important as the scoreboard.

The arena is a sealed rectangle of empty space beside the station, with no gravity and no way out but the pit hatches. Asteroids are towed in before each stage so there's something to hide behind.

The ships

The rules standardize the hardware: same hull, same thruster, same charge blaster, same 100 points of armor. What each owner customizes is everything else: the ship's name, its two colors and its core.

The core is the heart of the show. It's a small, short-lived payload, somewhere between a homemade firework and a jar of substances that do interesting things in zero gravity, and when a ship is knocked out its core breaks open and its pixels pour out. Every owner's recipe is different, so every knockout looks different.

The bonfire

The meet's prize is an honor, not a trophy: the winner presses the button on the bonfire, a megafirework with a far bigger payload than any ship carries. Everyone watches it together. If two pilots tie, they press it together.

Controls and interface

The whole game is played with one hand. Your ship always turns to face the mouse pointer, so aiming and steering are the same motion.

Fly

Mouse
Point to turn the ship to face that way
Right button
Hold to thrust forward

Shoot

Left button
Hold to charge the blaster (1 to 5 seconds)
Release
Fire, a quarter of a second later

There's no brake button and no need for one: the ship has a top speed and a gentle automatic air brake that slows it down as it drifts, so letting go of thrust is how you stop. The screen doesn't wrap around like in Asteroids; the arena has walls.

Game layers

  1. 01 · Moment-to-moment

    One stage

    A free-for-all among a handful of robo-ships in one arena, a few minutes long. You drift into position behind a rock, start charging, slide out, lead your target and let go, then duck back before someone else's charged shot finds you.

    Every ship starts with 100 armor. A knocked-out ship bursts into its core's pixels, its pit crew patches it up, and it relaunches from a pit hatch a few seconds later, so nobody spends a stage watching.

  2. 02 · Low metagame

    A meet

    A meet is a series of stages, three, six or nine, your choice, against the same field of contestants. Points carry over from stage to stage, and at the end the pilot with the most of them launches the bonfire.

    The longer the meet, the bigger the bonfire: each step up adds about a third more payload, so a nine-stage meet ends on the biggest show of all.

  3. 03 · High metagame

    The bonfire album

    Every bonfire is generated from a seed, so the game keeps each one in an album and can replay it exactly, pixel for pixel. Each entry records who won the meet and with how many points, whether that was you or one of the hobbyists.

    The field changes from meet to meet: every other contestant gets a generated pilot name, ship name and two-color scheme. There's always another meet, and the album is the station's history of them, not a list of things you've earned.

  4. 04 · Practical game flow

    A coffee break

    Start a meet, pick its length, and play. A stage is a few minutes; a three-stage meet fits a coffee break and a nine-stage one an evening.

    The game saves between stages, so a meet can be left and picked up later. Or skip playing altogether and open the album to rewatch old bonfires.

Features and mechanics

  1. The heart of it

    The charge blaster

    Every robo-ship carries the same single weapon. Hold the button to charge it, let go to fire, and a quarter of a second later a projectile leaves the nose at medium speed, straight ahead. The longer the charge, the harder it hits:

    ChargeDamageWhat it's for
    1 second5A quick poke, or knocking an asteroid out of the way
    In betweenScales upThe everyday trade-off between hitting now and hitting hard
    5 seconds60More than half a ship's armor in one hit

    That delay and the medium speed are what make it a game. With every ship drifting, you're always leading a target, and a full charge is a commitment: five seconds of being slow to react, in an arena where everyone can see you glowing.

  2. Falling sand

    Piñata cores

    When a ship's armor hits zero, it doesn't explode into debris. Its core breaks open and the ship turns into cellular-automata pixels, the falling-sand kind that behave like little particles of matter, each reacting to its neighbors.

    The pixels inherit the momentum of the hit, so they stream off in the direction the shot was going, and they start out burning hot neon before cooling into random dark, muted colors over one to five seconds, like fireworks. Then they drift off-screen or fade.

    Every core is its own recipe of colors and behaviors, so a knockout is a little show nobody has seen before. Asteroid breaks, blaster impacts and thruster exhaust make pixels too, on a smaller scale, so the arena always has something glittering in it.

  3. Cover that moves

    Asteroids

    Rocks drift around the arena between the ships and behave as they do in Asteroids: shoot one and it splits in two, and the halves split again, several times over, until nothing is left.

    Here they're cover, not a hazard. Bumping into one does no harm; it just gets pushed. So you can nudge a rock into a better spot, hide behind it while you charge, and let it soak up the shot meant for you, knowing it'll be a little smaller next time.

  4. Shared by everyone

    Power-ups

    Every fifteen seconds a power-up appears somewhere in the arena and bounces around until a ship touches it. It adds an ability to that ship's blaster for sixty seconds, and they stack, so a lucky pilot can end up with a very strange gun for a minute.

    Power-upWhat the shot does
    PhasePasses through asteroids and ships alike
    ScatterBursts on impact into six shrapnel hits at half power each
    HomingCurves slightly toward an opponent within 20 degrees of where it was fired
    WarpWraps around the arena's edge once, Asteroids-style
    TripleFires three shots in a spread, each at 75% power
    HypervelocityTwice the damage and twice the speed, but twice the charge time

    The computer-controlled hobbyists chase power-ups too, so a bouncing pickup pulls the whole field toward it, which is often the best moment to be charging behind a rock.

  5. Easy to learn, hard to master

    Drift flight on one hand

    Ships keep their momentum, turn to face the pointer, and thrust only forward, so flying means pointing where you want to go, pushing, and then turning around to shoot while you slide. The automatic air brake bleeds speed off gently, and a top speed keeps things readable.

    The skill is in combining it all with a shot that takes seconds to build: drifting sideways past a rock with the charge already full, turning at the last moment, and letting go where the other ship is going to be.

  6. A memento, not a prize

    Seeded bonfires

    Ship bursts and bonfires are generated, so instead of saving a video the game saves the seed that made each bonfire and replays it exactly, for next to no disk space. The album can hold every meet ever played.

    Each entry shows the winner and the score, and what you remember about the meet is your own story: the stage someone won with a homing triple shot, or the tie that ended with two pilots pressing the button together.

You're invited

Zero Space Robo Derby: Firework Arena Shooter

This month's meet is on. Bring your ship, your colors and whatever you've packed into your core this time. The hardware is standard, the asteroids are towed in, and the commentators have already mispronounced your name.

Charge it up, lead the drift, let go. Whatever happens, somebody's going to light up the arena.

Snacks at the hatch. Bonfire at the end.
