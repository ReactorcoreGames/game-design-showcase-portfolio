My definitions for words.

Once more, to avoid confusion, I want to list certain words and define them as I see them.

Design - An all-emcompassing term for a plan that defines what something is, how it functions, looks, feels and works. It also covers things that surround the subject of design, such as third party things like marketing, publishing, maintenance, support, updates and expansions. All of this and everthing that relates to a project is part of design.

Another definition for design is a roadmap or a strategic approach for someone to achieve a unique expectation. It defines the specifications, plans, parameters, costs, activities, processes and how and what to do within legal, political, social, environmental, safety and economic constraints in achieving that objective.


Game - An activity where play happens. Is not intended to have consequences in real life.

Play - Any human action doneto have fun with no serious intent to have real life impact.
Fun - Fulfillment of human needs. (See Manfred Maxneef's Human needs matrix.)


Designer - A person who creates design.

Developer - A person who uses art, code, sound and other technology to make design come to life. May also create design too.


Often people in the industry end up being both designers and developers, so the distinction can get blurry, so I may use the titles 'designer' & 'developer' interchangingly in this book.


Some technical terms next:

Game Mechanic - A chunk of interactive functionality in a game that focuses on a single thing and can be defined with a short label, such as "walking", "running", "crafting", "jumping", "shooting", "flying", "driving", "moving", "selecting choices/options", "commanding", "repairing", "communicating", "manipulating objects", "hiding/sneaking", "trading", "equipping/gearing up/outfitting/selecting tools", "resource gathering", "building", "painting", "destroying", "cleaning", "dreaming", "transforming", "possessing", "randomizing", "customizing", "modifying the environment", "augmenting vision", "organizing", "planning", "preparing", "fighting", "maintenance", "management" and many others.


Most of these are an action a player can do, but they also may be something the game itself does that affects how the player interacts with the game. A single mechanic only focuses on one thing and that alone, but it can interact with other mechanics to create combined results, such as "running" and "jumping". Together, a group of these game mechanics would become a system called "character movement" in this case.


Game System - A coherent collection of game mechanics and/or other various elements that interact, supplement or depend on eachother and have a common goal.


Couple examples of this would be "combat system", "movement system", "physics system", "weather system", "inventory system", "menu system", "progression system", "map system", "trading system", "customization system", "crafting system", "building system", "engineering system", "communication system", "online multiplayer", "chat system", "narrative story choice system" and infinitely many more.


None of these examples are definitive or always the same for each game, they're just to give an idea of whats the idea of the term "game system".


Game Structure - How the game is organized in its architecture. How the game is put together, how its designed, in both the sense of technical implementation and also in sense of the thinking/concept behind that implementation.


Game Feature - A collection of game mechanics, game systems and/or game structures that all contribute towards a single notable idea, that can be considered a remarkable, defining portion of a game. A big abstract chunk that defines the game, either partially or completely.


Game Content - I use this word very loosely. Game content can be practically anything, from a mechanic, system, structure, feature, artwork, graphics, sound, music, characters, items, story, layouts, maps, worlds, backgrounds, levels, blueprints, information, challenges, puzzles, ideas and more. The common thread among all these, is that they are consumable. They can be experienced, used, witnessed or enjoyed.


Often the word "content" is only used for only complete instances of objects that are in a game, such as characters, levels, items, vehicles and story related text, voice and choices. I percieve "content" far beyond that limited idea. I percieve and define it as anything that can I can consume as a player, anything that is remarkable to experience in the game, no matter how abstract it is. So, whenever you see me use the word "content", always assume the broadest possible definition.


Real-Life - Anything that happens outside of a video game world. Not meant as a derogatory term or an insult of any kind. Especially so as video game worlds do indeed affect real life, are inspired by real life and are an activity that exists within real life. This term is simply used to refer to situations that do not happen strictly ingame.

To clarify, this does not mean that Games cannot affect real life. Only that their *intention* is to primarily be a fantasy/noncommittal/fun activity and that they are designed from that outset intently. If a game's intention is to be something that affects real life, say an "education game" or "motivation facilitator", I wouldn't define it as a game at that point. It would be an interactive tool at that point. I want to be clear about this from the very start because its extremely important to understand the mindset how to approach game design and make good games.


Which brings me to following definition:

Gamification - Masking/pretending/faking an interactive tool as a game.


This becomes very important if your intention was to use games as a way to motivate enrichment of life in some way. I'll be explaining the effects of this and offering better alternatives further on. For things that operate like game, but are intended for real life consequencial purposes, like teaching, awareness and real life tool usage, I'll use this term:

Interactive Tool - An activity, software or a system that appears very similar to games in function, but their primary focus is on real life purposes, such as teaching, being a facilitative tool, a vessel for spreading awareness about some information or other serious real life purposes.

The Common Mistake


The common mistake:


When it comes to game design, the most easiest mistake to do is to approach a project with the mindset of a creating the final result, rather than manifesting that final result as a side effect created through a root cause.


To explain it in a different way, the common mistake is to try to take that initial vision/inspiration/fantasy/dream/wish/idea and create that directly, to directly imitate/mimic it as it is, instead of understanding the reasons behind it and using that information to create something that would eventually become the proper fulfillment of that vision/idea as a result.


The reason why this is a mistake is because the vision/idea is not guaranteed to bring fulfillment. Its merely a guess. It is only one of infinite strategies to bring fulfillment to the root causes, but it may be a flawed one. Creating a project in this way is blind.


With only the final result in mind, it does not give you the information whether each design decision is going to serve fulfilling the root cause behind your idea/vision. You are far more likely to make mistakes or commit bad design decisions that can actually make the project worse or be a useless waste of time.


However, the root cause, the understanding of real reasons behind the vision/idea, gives a clear idea that does actually give a real guarantee for fulfillment that a vision/idea is supposed to fulfill. Creating a project this way is confident, clear and effective.


The root cause is fundamental human needs - which I'll explain in detail in a later section - while video games or interactive tools are one of infinite diffirent strategies to meet those needs. Some strategies are poor, while other strategies are more effective. Strategies that connect with the root causes are most effective.


When the root cause is clearly understood, you have complete information to make the absolute best choice for every single design decision for your project. You'll know exactly what will be needed or not needed, allowing you to work more effectively and avoid wasting time, resources and effort on something that would otherwise have a bad or useless effect on the project. Understanding the root cause gives complete mastery over any project.


To further clarify what I mean, I want to list two examples. One for if you're creating a game and another for if you're creating an interactive tool.


Game example.

Lets say you were inspired by a game/movie/music/comic/book/nature/event/experience or anything similar and an idea/vision came to your mind from that, which involves creating a game.


The common mistake is to try to directly recreate the vision without first consulting what are root causes behind it. So if you saw a movie about exploring alien worlds, then the wrong thing to do would be to build alien worlds and create gameplay systems so you can repeat yourself what was done in the movie.


Instead, look at the root causes of the vision/idea. How did it make you feel? Then, using those feelings, you can pinpoint what those root causes are and notice what the real purpose of the project is. The specific process will be explained later on in this document. The root cause can alter development and design decisions a lot. The context the root cause will reveal can end up being any of the following:


  • About challenge, to improve yourself as a person, to grow spritually, intellectually or physically. Survive on the hazarous alien planet using your skill and limited resources.

  • About empowerment, to make you feel capable, confident and free. Conquer and manipulate the alien planet at your will with god like power.

  • About variety, to discover and explore something you had not previously been exposed to. To see something new. The alien planet is filled with mysteries and cool random things to discover and document.

  • About beauty and satisfaction, to get joy from seeing, hearing or doing something that pleases your senses. Move through out the alien planet, flying in it, interacting with it, seeing beautiful vistas, curious details, enjoying the experience of just being in it.


This is only some of the few possible root causes. Which ever root cause(s) it is, it can drastically alter what each part of the game will look like. Everything from the genre, aesthetical style, gameplay mechanics, UI design, marketing, control systems, narrative, context, theme. If you do not know the root cause, there is a high risk of accidently making a design decisions that conflicts with the purpose behind the vision or adding systems or content that either don't enrich the experience or make it worse, confused, messy.


If you actually do know the root cause however, you can avoid these mistakes and always have a clear compass to guide you towards the best design decisions for any aspect in the game at any time. No confusion, no wasted time. A direct efficient path that ensures your vision will actually satisfy you, instead of blindly missing the point and being a waste of time.


Interactive tool example.


Similarly, this about a real life issue, lets say you were inspired/troubled by a game/movie/music/comic/book/nature/event/experience or anything similar and an idea/vision came to your mind from that, which involves creating an interactive tool to solve it.


Again, the common mistake is to directly create a tool and offer it to a user without connecting with the root cause first. So if you're making a tool to bring awareness to global climate change or to teach math or teach a language or to teach cooperation or to teach a skill or technique, the wrong way to do it would be to just teach it directly.


The reasons why its wrong, is because the user has no context or reference of an experience as to why its important and how it would make life more wonderful. If the interactive tool only tries to teach without explaining why, the user cannot find the motivation to care about it. If it only tries to explain why and directly teach it, without demonstrating how it would make life more wonderful for the user, then it will only leave the user confused and concerned, merely knowing that its important, but unable to connect with it, lacking clarity on how it directly applies to their life nor how effective it is supposed to be. No reference to compare with.


Its important to point out that the reason you or someone is working on a project is exactly due to this context or reference of an experience.


You needed it to start and work on the project. Its essential. Its the very thing that drives you and makes you care about putting effort towards the project. Because of that context, you see the importance of why it needs to be made and shared.


Thing is, the user of the interactive tool needs that context or reference of an experience too to have the same passion to care about it. Its essential for the user too, not just the developer. Its vital for everyone involved, therefore it would be a dire mistake to ignore or forget it.


As such, looking at the root cause of the interactive tool and its intention is essential. The goal is to reveal the context or reference of an experience and then use that as the starting point and main driving force to create the interactive tool.


So instead of directly teaching/raising awareness/motivating towards something, give the user the same experience that made you emotionally care about it, while providing the teaching as an integrated experience along side of your personal inspiration event. Additionally, demonstrate clearly on how does the interactive tool concretely enrich the life of the user.


A couple quick examples:


For global climate change awareness, show what frightened you, how it made you feel, how life would be better for you if people would do what you advice them to do (your need is a gift). After that, show them within the context of your personal inspiration event, what to do and why clearly demonstrate why you think its effective.


For teaching math, again, show what inspired you, how it made you feel, how you're able to make life more wonderful with math. Show how life can be made wonderful for you if other people knew math and used it (your needs are a gift). Then, teach them math in context to what inspired you, allowing the user to concretely see why its effective and how to use it. Essentially your outspiration becomes inspiration for others. You outspire, others inspire what you outspire. Others breathe in what you breathe out. Thats what "inspiration" is.


For teaching cooperation, same thing. Share the experience that troubled you, how it made you feel, how life would be better for you if people cooperated together with others or with you. Teach and show them tools to cooperate, referencing to your experiences as the settings, since as a realistic example it shows how it concretely applies to real life and how to do it in a real life setting. It can be then expanded on how to use it elsewhere too, but not before the personal experience, as it is the strongest and clearest available for reference.


For teaching a skill/technique, once again same process. Your personal reference, your feelings, how it would make life wonderful for you. Then followed by showing and teaching the skill within the context of the personal reference. Finished of by demonstration of said skill/technique in action in a real situation to give a clear sense of reference that it works. The user would see its effectiveness and value on their own after this demonstration.


You may have noticed in the examples that I keep mentioning how to make life wonderful *for you* specifically, not for others. I also mentioned twice that "your needs are a gift". If you're confused by this or think of it negatively - such as it being a "selfish" thing - you'll soon find out that such a thing does not exist. This will be explained in detail in the section called "NVC", later in this document.

Unaware Beginner's approach:


First, I'd think of what I want to see in the game, based off what inspired me the most and what I think would be cool to have. So let me list those.

I'd think of the anime and remember what giant robots, guns, opponents, environment and scenes it had, so I'd definitely want all those recreated in the game. I'd refer to the anime for style guidance to decide how the aesthetics, graphics and the look and feel of the game would be like. I'd make sure that the music for the game would be of the same style as the genres I mentioned as well as the gameplay design focused between the connection with the music and the game. I'd look at existing traditions in existing games, compare older games and any other examples that I can copy popular ideas from or whichever ones I personally found attractive myself.


Most importantly, whatever my inspiration was, my goal as an unaware beginner would be to mimic it, to imitate it. I would try to bring it to life as I had seen it, as I had experienced it, as I had enjoyed it. I would not go into the source of the real reasons why I enjoyed it. I would not explore the root causes of what actually fueled the inspiration to be so powerful. I'd merely imitate the inspiration and try to copy its essence as I saw it.


I would then try to find a way to combine all of these things into a single product. Using previous knowledge and experience I have at my disposal, I would use that to determine if each combining idea was good or bad. I would use that information to decide any design decision that came. I would use that to try to understand what is missing from the game, so that I know what I need to add.


This previous knowledge and experience came through a lot of trial and error using this very method I'm describing right now, noticing flaws or strenghts in existing games I played, designed or observed. I'd remember these flaws and strengths and use this information to guide my decisions in future projects by comparing these old examples while I was creating something new, with the hope that doing so would make for a better game.


For reference:

By "bad" or "flaws" I mean anything that seems to make the game worse.
By "good" or "strenghts" I mean anything that seems to make the game better.


Its important to emphasize that I was only able to see these flaws or strenghts AFTER a project was complete and I had tried it. This approach offer no capability to notice these flaws or strenghts before they are implemented unless I already knew the answer from previous experiences.

It also does not offer any method to understand why something was a flaw or a strenght. There neither was any ability to detect where a flaw or a strenght truly originates from. I might look at something and think it is a flaw or a strenght, but the complicated interconnected system of human/hardware/game that make up a playing experience can easily fool me, due to a lack of methods to verify my guess if something truly is a flaw or a strenght.


I may think that adding more guns, more robots, more explosions, multiplayer, leaderboards, punishments and rewards, character customization, an open world, a progression system with leveling, permadeath, more content, more enemies, more environments, an epic story, more realism, voice acting and better, higher resolution graphics and effects will make for a better game. Just because tradition and popularity says so or that it seems like the most obvious thing that other games would do and seemed better for it.


Likewise due to traditions and popularity, I may think negatively about grinding, overpowered content, microtransactions, short lenght of game experience, simpler graphics, simpler effects, lack of narrative or story, less content, lower resolution textures, low fidelity audio, simplified user interface, lack of innovation or revolutionary additions, singleplayer cheating, multiplayer cheaters, lack or removal of common popular or traditional features/mechanics, disregard certain platforms, styles, aesthetics, narratives, themes, ideas, concepts and genres as inferior or bad and more. All these just because they're common, considered traditional, popular or simply because someone just said so.

This approach is the easiest, most convenient and obvious thing to do. "Someone already did something seemingly right, therefore its safe to follow their advice, copy the same features they had and expect it will work fine". The problem is, this method is also horrible, even suicidal. I'm only looking at the surface, the face value of whatever I'm trying to replicate, instead of understanding the root causes behind or underneath it. I'm completely blind and have no idea what I'm actually doing when I design anything this way.


I might get lucky enough to accidently stitch together just the right amount of good design decisions to make a project be above average, but I'm missing out on gigantic amount of potential with no awareness of it, let alone any ability to harvest it. Replicating any success would also be increasingly difficult with this approach with each subsequent project.

I am not truly in touch with my needs, feelings, inspiration or purpose for the project. Only my gut feeling, traditions, popular culture and common information are all I have to guide me. Unfortunately that is not enough. I have no methods to verify my design decisions nor have a clear approach to find out what is missing, useless, required, detrimental or optional for the project.


To an unaware beginner, this might seem like a great approach that makes obvious sense and surely produces real results. Unfortunately the reality is opposite. It tricks you into bad desicions, makes you less likely to verify ideas on your own and produces blind results at worst.



Experienced Specialist's approach:


This next part may sound wierd until you read the Mindset/NVC part further in this document, so feel free to scroll back up here to re-read this a second time after seeing that.


The first thing I do is remind myself that playing or making video games is a strategy to meet/satisfy fundamental human needs. Nothing more, nothing less. I already have the knowledge of Nonviolent Communication to know what this means and what needs there are in total, so that is a prerequisite that I'm able to do this realization.


Looking at the inspiration and the resulting vision/idea that came from it, I would try to ask myself how both of these make me feel. What feelings does the inspiration give me, what feelings does the vision/idea give me? In this case "inspiration" being the anime of the giant robots blowing things up (plus the music thing I mentioned), while the "idea/vision" being my plan to create a video game based on that anime/experience/inspiration to satisfy the need that the inspiration brough up in me.


Feelings are an automatic natural function of the human body that point towards fundamental human needs, so I next try to use my feelings I detected previously to connect them with my needs. Some are obvious and I can name them instantly, while others I can determine through the process of elimination. I can use the official NVC material or Manfred Maxneef's human needs matrix to see all the available needs. NVC also provides a handy list of possible feelings if I ever feel unsure of what my feeling exactly is.


So my vision/idea/want is to pilot the giant robots and blow things up, fly fast, dodge hazards, interwoven with the experience to listening to energetic music. I want to feel powerful, free and unrestricted. I want to discover and be surprised by unpredictable situations in the game. I want the whole experience to have ongoing momentum, where you could enter a "flow state" and keep enjoying the game without abrupts breaks in gameplay that would otherwise terminate this momentum and the flow state associated with it. I want to be able to play this game infinitely, but I also want it to be meaningful and fresh/new/unexpected each time I play it. I'd like it to be easy to pick up at any time and get a complete experience of a campaign in about 30-60 minutes. I want the game to respect me as a human being, meaning not wasting my time, never removing my autonomy, keeping me informed of the real world (reminders to drink, eat, sleep, pause, show real world time ingame) and generally empowering me instead of enslaving me.


All of these ideas/visions/wants came from various sources of inspiration. Some of those showed new things I could do, others showed things that left me wanting for something better. My mind collected them automatically, stored them in my brain and then finally connected them together by chance. This happened through a lot of procrastination and exploring and experiencing everything around me, both physically in the real world and on the internet. Anything I saw or was exposed to in my life previously, was collected into my ever-growing library of references in mind. Thats the place where I get ideas from and form judgements from.


Looking at my vision, I can notice feelings on both sides. What feelings are driving me to create it and what feelings I want to see this game provide me. I'm feeling frustration, disgruntlement, annoyance because this solution does not exist yet or is flawed, so I can't satisfy my needs the way I'd like to. I also feel excitement, inspiration, hope that this project can actually be a real chance at making my life more wonderful. From the game itself, I'm expecting to feel stimulated, amazed, empowered, joyful, energetic, lively, awed, strong and free.


Translating the feelings into fundamental human needs, its pretty obvious that from this game project specifically, I have a need for freedom/autonomy, power/capacity, challenge, variety/novelty/surprise and play/joy.


Confirming to myself that this is indeed what needs I want to get met by this project, I have now found a focused purpose to create a game that satisfies these needs. If not, then I'll keep exhausting the feelings and needs involved until I have confirmed them all. I cannot continue with creating the project until I have fully listed all the feelings and needs that are at the root of a project. I must make sure that the purpose is as clear as possible.


Suddenly, I had just acquired the most powerful tool in the world to ensure my game design will be the best possible for this project. I now have clear understanding for what I truly want, allowing me to quickly determine any design decision always towards the best option available. The purpose of the project is clear.


I now have the power to completely bypass traditions, popular memes, prejudices and dependancies on other peoples opinions. I can think myself with my own brains and come up with real effective solutions myself, no matter what the situation is, nor how small or big a particular thing is. I can completely avoid making mistakes that I'd otherwise only learn from afterwards through trial and error. I can see if something will work before even trying it! I can avoid accidently making the game worse through bad design decisions that run contrary to the games purpose.


Other benefits include having a clear, endless source of motivation and meaning, total thought independence and ownership of the project, a handy compass that always points towards the best design decision option at any given time. I can also look at existing games that seemed to be successful and truly understand what and why made them successful, down to the very tiniest detail. It does not get better than this!


Starting from the very mundane basics, I will start creating the design for the game.


Will it be 2D or 3D or other? Which camera angle or view would be the best to use? What platform would be the best to develop this game on? What technology will I use? What type of graphics will I use? What complexity/size/scope will this game be? How will the game work? What will the game have? How will content be created for this game? How will that content work in this game? Will it be a video game, a board game, a card game, an outdoor activity taking place in real life, an interactive movie or something completely different?


These general questions and any other similar ones that come up are now answerable without hesitation. The questions themselves arise naturally out of necessity, especially when you actually want to start building a game straight away during the rush created by the combined inspiration and vision, only to suddenly finding yourself facing the nitty gritty of trying to make an actual game and discovering the complications in the details.


For each question, I would go and find out all the possible options that are available. I'd search, ask, look around, research and find however I can, both in real life and on the internet to assemble all the possible and probable choices that I can have for each question. I would also listen to my bodily feelings, my gut reaction, the assess both the options I have found and if I feel that I should keep looking for more options.


I would then ask myself, "which one of these options/choices serve the purpose best?". If my answer is "I don't know" for one or more of the available options, then I will test them, experiment them and try them in the most fastest, easiest, cheapest, low cost way I possibly can. The point is simply to gather information, so any of these tests are temporary, disposable experiments that require no more effort than what is necessary.


For example, "2D side view or 3D third person view: which one suits my project the best?"

I would try to imagine in my mind how the game would look and feel in both views. I would look up existing 2D and 3D games to see how they work and feel. I would make cheap/crappy doodles with pen and paper to help imagining both options in every situation I could think of.


Since I wanted speed and high maneuverability and I'm wanting to satisfy my need for freedom, I'd eventually find out that a 2D view is more restrictive, less free due to the fact I cannot see what is infront of me far away and the distance to notice something on a limited side view screen is too claustrophobic to notice fast objects coming in fast enough to react to them. Meanwhile, 3D third person view allows me to look far beyond into the horizon, towards infinity, allowing me to see objects way farther than regular 2D side view could ever hope to show. I can also react to objects coming form afar much easily, since I can see them way before I could in a 2D side view.


As such, the 3D third person view feels better, because its more likely to meet my need for freedom, speed and control that I want from this game. With that, I just found out the best possible option out of those two choices.


The process for each question is always the same.

  1. Lay out all the possible options on the table you can possibly find, any way you can.

  2. Get familiar with each option available on the table.

  3. Compare each option with the purpose of the project, individually.

  4. Choose the option that serves the purpose of the project better than the other ones.


Use the lowest, cheapest methods to experiment, test and familiarize with the options you are not familiar with. Its ok to be unaware of something and say "I don't know", but after that its not ok to ignore it and not take the time to learn about it. Always say "I don't know, but i'll find out" instead of "I don't know, therefore I'll ignore it forever".


Also be aware that we are always limited to what is available to us, so if something is possible or exists, but no one told you about it, nor is there any convinient way for you to find out about it, then that option, no matter how amazing or poor it is to serve the purpose, is unavailable as an option. It will not exist until discovered. For this reason, its a good idea to explore, experiment, test and go to places no one has ever gone before in order to find these unavailable options. Potentially they can be revolutionary, so keep an eye out and keep your mind open to unknown possibilities.


To help handling the information regarding deciding on the options, do not hesitate to use anything that helps ease the load of information on your brain by transferring it to paper, documents, doodles, cheap drawings, text or any other way you can. The process of answering each question can be an exhausting one as you gather information and then try to compare it to find the best option. Information overload is a real risk that can happen easily, leaving you unable to think or take on new information until you've unloaded the information you're holding in your head into a document or drawing. From those documents or drawings, you can reference it without fear about unintentionally forgetting and losing that information forever. In the process of design, its unfortunately very easy to forget important information by accident.


This risk becomes increasingly larger, the more complicated the project is, so make sure to transfer your thoughts on to paper regularly and create back ups. And do so in a way that you're sure you will remember the information correctly when you do view that document or drawing in the future, as its a common mistake to be too vague, unspecific while creating a document or drawing, and as a result be completely clueless what your reference means when you pick the document/drawing again after a couple months later in the future, unable to recall the memory at all.


Be sure to keep all the scribbles, notes, drawings, doodles, documents and other material in a single, safe place that you can always find them intact when you will need them. Its a horrible feeling to know that you've recorded information for reference, but have that piece of reference lost, destroyed or missing when you really need it.


Using this very process, I can answer any question, of any complexity, of any topic, of any level of detail, of any size or significance, to arrive at the sum total of a complete design for my giant robots shooting lasers in space game.


Going deeper, using methods and tools that I'll be showing in the Technical Side section, numerous question will arise during the project, such as:


  • What style/aesthetic/theme would be the best for this project?

  • What is the best structure for user interface for menus and hud elements?

Using the method I described, finding the best answer to each of these question and more is no problem whatsoever, with absolute confidence. The answer may often not be instantaneous, but it is always inevitable and confident. You may also notice that the questions themselves extend far beyond the game itself, towards the things that surround the project, such as marketing, monetization and management. Contrary to popular belief, these are an essential part of game design too.


I also want to mention that some design question can only become available and answerable once other questions have been answered. In game design, many things depend on one another or are interconnected, so to answer a question like "what color should the main menu be?", I must first have answered yes to the question "does the game need a main menu?" before that.


These questions are only a tiny portion and not in any specific order, just as a general demonstration of what type of questions might arise for this specific project. I must stress that these questions arise out pure necessity from the very specific vision only unique to this project. They are for this project, for its specific purpose ONLY. Under no circumstances, absolutely never copy these questions to use for another project directly. If the questions do not come up naturally out of necessity during design development, then they must not be arbitrarily forced upon that project at any cost. Doing otherwise can seriously damage design efforts and the final product. Remember that the point here is to use your own brain instead of being a blind slave to someone elses arbitrary commands.


At the core of it all, its all about "Does this serve the purpose of the project?" or "Does this get me closer to satisfying the purpose of the project?"


Thats the only question you need to ask. Check the purpose. Check the needs behind the purpose. Check how you feel about it. Do they match up with an option you are looking at? If yes, do it. If not, do not it. If you don't know, test it and gather information until you do.


Focus only on the purpose. If a feature is essential to fulfilling the purpose, then the project cannot be successful without it. If its not relevant, secondary or even opposite, its inclusion will be a disaster for the project and waste your time.


Its all about meeting fundamental human needs using a purpose-driven strategy that was created by the inspiration, which in turn spawned the vision of the strategy itself. Once the purpose is crystal clear, you can refer to it in the face of any design decision that the project's creation will throw at you.

My project: Creating an improved version of something old


This one is going to be less specific like the last example, as this situation is something that has happened multiple times both for myself and while closely observing others, so instead of focusing on just one example, I want to cover them all. These will cover a very wide array of project types. The only similar thing is that the project is still created from the very start and that its about being inspired by another project from elsewhere.


Its the process for creating a Sequel, Reboot, Remake, Prequel, Expansion, Remix, Redux or any other project that was inspired by another project. No specific example, just the general idea.


Much of the design process is very similar to the one described previously in the "creating something completely new" section, both for the Unaware Beginner as well as the Experienced specialist. The same dynamic of the beginner following traditions, looking at whats is popular, putting things together via enhanced imitation/mimicry of the original inspiration and hoping blindly that it will work out fine. Likewise, the specialist will find out the inspiration behind the project, as well as the feelings and needs involved and use those to guide the design. All that stays the same. The only difference is that instead of starting from nothing, just a mere inspiration and an idea that came from it, now there is something existing that I can start from and have to take into account.


Again, I won't present a specific example, but explain the process in general for both sides. For this, I'll assume I'm making a sequel to a game I liked in the past.


Unaware Beginner's approach:


I would start by analysing the old project, looking at it at face value, trying to see what features it has that I particularly enjoyed or thought were good. I would also look at the features, systems and mechanics I did not like or through were bad.

By "good", I mean the feature contributed to the project positively. Make the game better.

A couple examples of this would be:


  • A certain art-style that looked pleasing to the eye. Its use of shapes, colors and tone contributed to creating an atmosphere for the game's universe that felt like it gave a sense of "realness". This made it both easy to accept it as a plausible reality to easily be accepted by my human mind and immersed in it and also to feel a sense of wonder at how remarkable and interesting the world it is.

  • A mechanic in the game when a person is killed with overwhelming force, they explode into smaller chunks that represent pieces of meat with bones and particle effects that represent a spray of blood. Accompanied with squishy, meaty, impactful sound effects. It feels satisfying as it gives a sense like the interactivity between a source of danger (projectiles, bullets, swords, melee weapons, explosives etc) and the target (human, animal, robot, alien, object) feel more tactile, more real. It feels like this is what I would expect to happen in real life, even if its not realistically accurate.

  • An overworld map that shows the game's world from a high top-down perspective. It gives a better understanding of what the surrounding world is like, giving a sense of meaning and clarity. If the game has a linear campaign that takes place across this world map, I also get a sense of progression and achievement that I'm actually going somewhere and getting something important done.

  • Any type of upgrade/experience/leveling/progression system that directly or indirectly makes my character stronger, different, deeper or richer as I keep playing the game further. I get the sense of empowerement and meaning as I do whatever the game asks me to do, validating that what I did was in the right direction and recieved reinforcement to keep doing that more.

  • Multiplayer functionality that allow two or more people from across the world play the same instance of a game together. The benefits differ for each game, with competitive games gaining an element of an intelligent, surprising and sociable opponent, while collaborative and cooperative games gain surprising, sociable and intelligent companions. In both cases, the game becomes more interesting, remarkable, surprising by the inclusion of humans. Humans can also do things and behave in ways that artificial intelligence may not be able to, making the gameplay more technically advanced than its technology would otherwise allow.

  • Big, visually impressive explosions. They feel satisfying to look at. It may be a simple thing, but it does make the game feel more epic than it would without big explosions.



By "bad", I mean the feature felt like it damaged the experience. Made the game worse.

A couple examples of this would be:


  • Game interface and controls are too complicated. This is something that a casual gamer would find bad. They have limited time to play games, so if a game presents a complicated user interface and equally difficult control scheme to play the game, it may pose a significant enough difficulty to learn the game. This can come off as intimidating, unnecessary and troublesome for the casual player, prompting them to leave the game with disappointment.

  • Microtransaction or a cash shop, where the player can use real money to buy items. Either cosmetic vanity items that have no gameplay effect or upgrades, boosters or even cheats to make the game easier or give an unfair advantage over other players. This is something a long-time hardcore gamers would find irritating. In the past, most of the things used to be free and included with the game for the entry price it cost and implemented in a way that respects the game's internal balance of power and fairness. Breaking this trust renders a game unenjoyable to the hardcore gamer.

  • Unskippable ingame tutorial that assumes people are unaware of very basic things, even if they actually are unaware of them. This irritates people because they came to have fun, yet the game presents an arbitrary barrier in the player's way that prevents them from getting to the actual gameplay. Even more so this is irritating to returning players who want to play the game again, as they are forced to view a tutorial to learn things they already know, thus effectively wasting their time and patience for no reason.

  • Grinding or a repetitive, boring, monotonous ingame action that is not satisfying in itself nor is done out of a desire to do it for its own merit, but which is done to achieve or gain something extrinsic as a result of it. Having to do grinding in a game is absurb in itself. The point is to have fun, get engaged and entertained, yet the player is doing the very opposite of that as they grind.

  • A mechanic, feature or system that feels like a gimmick, something special/quirky/unique for the sole purpose of being special/quirky/unique. These types of features are often contrived and arbitrary, meaning that they violate the consistency and standards within the game universe they exist, damaging immersion for the player and thus making the game difficult to be taken seriously. The effect of a gimmick is that the game is essentially cheating or breaking the fourth wall (immersion/wholeness) against itself.


I would then think of ways to keep the "good" features in and find ways to remove "bad" features. While keeping the good features in, I would consider how to make those features bigger, more advanced, more complex in hopes of achieving a better product than the original one. I would also look outside to find what people or myself think is "good" by popularity, traditions and requests. Upon finding such features I would think of ways to integrate them into the project, with the intention to make this new game better, more advanced, more superior and bigger.

This is a dire mistake. My selection and improvement process for features I'd like to see in the game or improving the existing features has no foresight whether they will fulfill the purpose of the project or not. The ideas and design desicisions during this process may sound and feel effective to an unaware beginner, but they only do so because there is a lack of knowledge of the root causes that otherwise would reveal why this is such a poor approach to design. Things that are popular or traditional are not guaranteed to be compatible, effective or beneficial in other projects. They can damage or even destroy the project instead! Be aware that a frighteningly large number of game developers, including many of the very prominent ones, have no idea what they are doing. Following them without question is like blind leading the blind.


A huge thing to notice is that the original seemingly enjoyable old game had a certain mindset while its features and systems were selected and developed for it (Atleast usually that is the case, unless the game was accidently designed well through intuition). The problem is that the new game - as approached by an unaware beginner - has no understanding of the importance of the original mindset, nor its purpose. As an unaware beginner, I wouldn't think of that original purpose, I'd think I know what that purpose is by looking at the game at face value and deducing by the way it works, instead of thinking about what inspired it. I'd think of only improving the game by looking at it at face value instead of considering the root causes underlying the design decisions of the original game.

As a result, I might be confident that the features I'm removing are truly safe to remove and that they indeed are the result of making the game bad and not something else entirely, while adding in features that I consider good (and I even witnessed them seemingly work well for other games) will actually be compatible with the old game's design and make it better.


The situation is also complicated from another angle too. If the original developer was developing the old game as an unaware beginner, yet came up with a design that seems like it works, another unaware beginner who tries to create a new game based on the old game might accidently think that the original design was flawless or well designed, unaware that certain features, mechanics, systems and their combined result are actually not created out of a purpose-based mindset, meaning they most likely are not the best possible solution for that game. A lot of great potential can be missed this way quite easily, creating a game that is only a mere shadow of what it can be.

Not only that, but this unachieved or wasted potential can be noticed by the players (which eventually becomes more apparent the more a player keep playing a game or has played similar games in the past), can make them have thoughts that lead to feelings of sadness and frustration, because they needed more wholeness from the game, causing greatly diminished enjoyment to the point of even ruining the entire experience for them, making the players leave the game.


As an unaware beginner, even if I might accidently make an engaging game by doing this approach, but I'd only be scratching the surface of what is possible. I also won't truly know why it turned out engaging, believing the improvements I made were the real reason for the success, while being unaware to the real root causes.

Otherwise, this development strategy is largely blind and can disasterously end with me making an end result that is worse than the original old game and not even truly know why it failed. Either way, its not the best possible way to approach a project.



Experienced Specialist's approach:


Instead of looking at the old project at face value, the features, systems, mechanics and structure that it may have or seem to be lacking, I would try to listen to what the purpose of the old project was.

The most direct way to find the purpose is by asking the original creator what were they needing and feeling when they created the game. I'd keep collecting as much information as possible to find out what inspired them, why they chose certain design decisions over others and what were their expectations of the old project they were building back then. I'd try to hear the human being behind the project to understand why they were creating the project and what they were hoping from it.


Be aware that there is a real possibility that even the original creator of the old project is unaware of the purpose, having created something without fully understanding it themselves. They may either have had a subconcious intuition that guided them, but do not have the words to explain it, let alone even conciously realize why they did so. They may also have brute-forced their way through the method of trial and error until something that resembles what they were looking for was created as a happy accident.

It is also very possible that the project was developed for an extrinsic purpose, putting less concern on the game than what it represents or gives to the creator, but still coming up with something that inspires me. As an example, the project's purpose was to simply experiment and do personal improvement growth of skill, knowledge or creativity. The creator was merely using the game project as a stepping stone for advancing oneself, than to create a great game worth playing in itself. The primary goal was not concerned with the game itself, but the benefit that it would provide, be it growth, learning, money, influence, social acceptance, community, playful experimentation to satisfy curiosity, enjoy the fun of development process than the product itself and so on, while anything else that came out of it was a side effect, either intentional or unintentional.


In that case, the game itself was secondary, meaning the developer does not truly care for it in the same way a developer that had the game as their primary priority (making the game for intrinsic reasons). If this is the case, then their purpose will not be useful to me, because there is a disconnect with what I was inspired versus what the original creator's intention for the old project was.


For this reason, or in the event that the original creator is not available to talk with for whatever reason (be it reluctance, busy, unreachable or dead) and as a personal investigation tool, I can find the purpose myself by analysing the game itself. If the original creator's purpose was extrinsic, then the purpose I might find will be different, as it will be based on the merit of the game itself, rather than an external reason. This particular purpose is a more personal one, about what I myself want from the game instead of what its original intentions are, if there were any.


Just a small side note first. If your intention is to actually follow the extrinsic purpose as the original creator's real purpose was, then you will find it very difficult to get any good information that would help you creating an engaging product. It would be developing a game that you are not interested in playing, yet somehow expecting others to be interested in it. That path leads to ruin, do not take it.

One last thing to take into account is the situation at hand. What is the intention of the new developer? Do I want to preserve the old design at all or am I open to overhaul the whole project to satisfy its purpose in a different way entirely? If the old project was well designed with the experienced specialist's approach, then this will only be a matter of preference. However if it is not, then for the most part the only option is overhaul, unless there are pieces of accidentally suitable design that clearly serves the purpose that the game was aiming for(or the purpose you felt its meant to serve). Those suitable parts can be salvaged or serve as a possible idea worth using if the rest of the design works well with it.

So now I want to explain how I can find the purpose of a game by myself. Instead of analysing the mechanics, features and systems of the old project like the unaware beginner would, I would play the game, explore my experience and inspiration of it by asking these things:


  • What I'm feeling when I play the game?

  • What do certain parts of the game make me feel?

  • What does each detail in the game make me feel?

  • What do I want to feel from this game?

  • What do I want certain parts to feel life?

  • What do I want each individual detail in the game feel like?

  • (If developing for someone other than you)
    What do they want to feel for from the game, its parts and its details?

    I would write this information down in whatever way I feel most comfortable, be it pen and paper or a text document on a computer or any other medium I feel is most suitable to capture the information I discover. The point is to record this information so I can always come back to reminds myself about these feelings. This is very important information I'm gathering here, because they greatly help me in making informed decisions for the design of the new game that accurately will contribute to its intended purpose. These feelings will work as a compass that always points towards the best possible solution when reviewing ideas.


The crucial thing about the feelings, is that they reveal satisfied or unmet fundamental human needs. Again, this will be explained in the section titled "NVC" further on. The point is, I'm focusing on my own feelings and needs to understand my inspiration of the old game in order to discover either the old game's purpose or my personal purpose that I want from the old game.

As such, I'll use the purpose to guide my design decisions and also use to it analyse the flaws and strenghts of the old and new designs. Else's project: Working on an existing project to make it better


In this situation I'm joining as a partner/outsider to work with a lead developer or a team of developers who are already working on a project. My task is to help with game design, entering at the start of the project or when its already in progress. I will be analysing the existing plan and then either provide feedback on how to improve it or to take over the entire design as my responsibility and ensure that it will be succesful. By successful, I mean that it will meet the requirements and desires set by the people I'm working with.


Said in a more plain language, my task is to give them what they want or ask for. They have a dream of some sort (a need) and probably have an existing concept (a strategy) that they believe will (or could) achieve that dream.


This is not my project, nor my initiative. I'm joining in either as an enthusiast or as a contractor for an extrinsic reason. The priority of the other persons who own the project is the highest. I'm there to help them achieve what they are dreaming.


Here are some of the situations when I may arrive to help:


  • During the start of a project.

  • During active development with an existing prototype and existing plans.

  • During beta testing with a mostly complete game.

  • During launch where the game is complete, but will recieve updates.

  • At post mortem to provide information why something works or does not work.


Each situation is different, but I can't particularly say if one is less enjoyable than the other. One might think that the start of a project is the best place to come in, since thats where no actual game production has started, thus making it the most flexible and open environment held back by nothing. However, if the developers are open to killing or overhauling the entire project in the light of evidence that reveals a faulty design headed for failure in the prototype, beta or a complete game, then the situation is as open and flexible as it would be if I were to enter during the start of a project.


Then again, an existing design can be helpful or annoying. If there are certain beliefs, traditions, culture or any other things that the team or lead developer revere, then the process of convincing and explaining may be a difficult task. However, it may be that the existing design has laid a functional framework that is worth using or has elements that may be useful in some way, even if just as a warning sign that may be helpful during the mentioned "convincing" thing, why a particular design may not be effective to meet the dream of the team, then coming in during middle or end of development can be not any worse than coming in during the start, maybe sometimes even better so.


Like I said, each situation will be different and will need an individual approach for each of one them. Some may be more difficult than others, but its not practical to generalize one situation as superior or inferior to another one. There is no "good" or "bad". There is only "different", as each situation will present its own unique challenge with certain things that are in favor of me, while others are against me.

I also want to highlight that providing game design or design consultation is a position of great responsibility and power. What will I say and design will affect everyone on the team, as game design dictates every aspect of the product, from code, art, audio, marketing and design itself, making design the single most important aspect of any project. As such, everyone around me will be putting a lot of trust in me, so I want to value that and make sure that my partners will get their needs met with the same high importance as my own needs. I need to be able to detect this dangerous situation and be ready to translate and verify what they ask for versus what they actually want. This will require honest, open, but firm observation of how I see the situation without judgement, in order to bring awareness when a certain existing or proposed strategy for design is or may be detrimental to the project.


Again, I'll be speaking generally without a specific example as I can cover more situations at once without being constrained to a single project type.


That said, you'll see that the same pattern as the past two examples showed, apply here all the same; The unaware beginner tries to experiment their way blindly with solutions that seem appealing, but fail to see the root causes that would otherwise reveal if their solution will be effective, useless or detrimental. Meanwhile the experienced specialist focuses on the purpose through feelings and needs, creating tailored solutions to all design problems with absolute clarity and confidence.


Unaware Beginner's approach:


I'd start off with two important things. First, I'd look at any existing material and see what is it representing. Second, I'd ask and gather as much detailed information as possible from the partners I am working with, to understand what they want to create and what they want from me.


With the first part, "what any existing material is representing", I mean want I to see what existing work, doodles, notes, documents, images has already been created. I also want playtest any prototypes, demos or experiments if there are any of those.


After seeing all that existing material, I want to take an educated guess at what my partner's intention may be for this project. These are my own personal thoughts:


  • What do I personally think is being created here?

  • Why do they want to do this? (I take a guess)


This will be helpful in the second part, where I'll be comparing with the answers I get about the project with whatever existing work they may have already created.


In the second part, these are some of the questions I'd ask from my partners to understand what were supposed to create:


  • What are you trying to create?

  • Why do you want to make it?

  • What inspired/prompted you to create it?

  • How would you like the final result to be?

  • What would you consider as success for this project?

  • What would you consider as failure for this project?


Aside from these questions, I will probably need to keep asking "why?" to follow up on the answers I get, as I predict the answers will either be vague, dismissive, incomplete or not the answer I was hoping to get. I cannot expect others to be skilled enough in game design or with communication, meaning my partners may not know what is important to mention or they may not know how to explain that important information in words, so I'll need some patience to translate their given answers until get complete information I requested from them. I'll need to go deeper and keep asking until I fully understand what they really want from the project.


Clear and full communication with my partners is critical for a team project success. Even if I may have better ideas than they do, I predict they will be resented and I will have great difficulty in making any progress with the project if I do not connect and listen to my partners before trying to realize these ideas. I must never try meet my own needs while ignoring other peoples needs, otherwise it will extremely difficult to maintain a compassionate relationship with trust, willingness to help one another and to listen to each other.


I'd use these additional questions to drill deeper to further my understanding of what the plans for the project are:

  • "Why do you think this will work?"

(Can you explain it to me in your own words, as I have a need for better understanding as to what the logic behind this thought/idea is? How did you come to that conclusion?)


  • "How would you achieve that?"

(I'm unsure how is this thing going to solve that thing. Can you clarify the connection for me on how will this work? How certain are you about the success of this strategy? I have need for clarity and certainty in this. Please explain.)


Using these, I can verify for myself if an idea sounds good or not based on its logic compared to the intention of my partners. If the logic of an idea seems to fit with what the project is supposed to achieve, then there is a high chance of success for that idea.


Sadly, without having a previous experience of an idea, like seeing it in action elsewhere and that it is too complicated to discern how it will turn out, there may be flaws hidden within it that are too subtle to notice. Thankfully this problem can be mitigated almost completely by testing the idea out, either by creating a live experiment to see it perform in the most fastest, cheapest, crudest, basic form possible or to use pen and paper to create a mind map or a mind dump that explores any uncertainties that I may not be 100% sure about.


By a mind map/mind dump, I'm referring to simply thinking about the idea, writing the idea itself down, then writing any thoughts that feel unknown or uncertain, writing down any noticable pros and cons of this idea, writing down possible alternatives and listing their own pros and cons, writing down anything that seems even slightly remarkable about the idea in the effort of understanding the idea and all its aspects as fully as possible. Its completely free-form, no rules or restrictions. The whole point is to dump as much information that is in my mind onto paper so that I can make sense of it more efficiently and organize it better. Doing so will allow me to easily see possible problems and benefits of an idea before even testing it out live.


After all this information gathering and clarification, I then begin to think how I can contribute to the project in order to start it, complete it or enhance it, depending on when I've arrived on the team to work on the project based on what stage of development it is currently at.


Up until this point, these are exactly the same steps an experienced specialist would do as well. However from here on the paths between the unaware beginner and the experienced specialist go in different directions.


Again, as the unaware beginner, I would look for existing examples, traditions and popular trends to inform my decisions and thoughts on what I should do in order to fulfill the desire of the team for the project were creating together.


As usual, this is where things start getting worse for the unaware beginner. There is no understanding of fundamental human needs, therefore no clearly defined purpose can be found, as the understanding of fundamental human needs is a requirement to do so. There is no method to verify design decisions to know if they truly work or not. Chances of success for the entire project are random.


I may make lucky decisions resulting in accidental or conservative success, but even then I'll only know the results once the project has been published. At that point its already too late to fix the core design and the sequel, if there will be one, is doomed to repeat the same blind cycle.



Experienced Specialist's approach:


Up until the part where the unaware beginner begins to create the actual design or otherwise contribute to the project after gathering all the needed information using the questions mentioned earlier, the experienced specialist does all of this too.


The key difference is a closer focus on fundamental human needs and the desired feelings the finished product is supposed to deliver on.


At the beginning, I would start with looking at the existing material the same way an unaware beginner would, asking myself what is trying to be made here and why, taking educated guesses. However, I wouldn't stop there. In addition to those two questions, I'd ask these:


  • What needs is this project trying to fulfill?

  • What is the project trying to make me feel?

  • Why do my partners want to meet these needs in this particular way?

  • Why do my partners think that this strategy will be effective?


  • Again, these are questions aimed at myself for me to make an educated guess based on my own personal opinion. The answers I arrive at will be useful when I compare them with the answers I'll get from my partners to the six core questions, which were these:

  • What are you trying to create?

  • Why do you want to make it?

  • What inspired/prompted you to create it?

  • How would you like the final result to be?

  • What would you consider as success for this project?

  • What would you consider as failure for this project?


Once again, I don't stop here either. When I ask these questions as an experienced specialist, my goal is to understand the feelings and needs of my partners that drive the answers I recieve. My aim is to uncover what going on beyond the logical thinking that their answers reveal. As such, if I need to, I'll expand these question with secondary questions like this:


  • What are you trying to create?
    What feeling(s) is it supposed to deliver?

  • Why do you want to make it?
    What need(s) is it supposed to fulfill in you?

  • What inspired/prompted you to create it?
    That inspiration, what feelings and needs did it touch in you?

  • How would you like the final result to be?
    How will this evoke the desired feeling(s)? How will it fulfill the need(s)?

  • What would you consider as success for this project?
    What is your method of judgement to know if your project has achieved its goals?

  • What would you consider as failure for this project?
    What do you want to avoid from happening?


These secondary questions will provide much more accurate information on what the partners are really striving for, as now the dialog is focused on feelings and the fundamental human needs behind them. Without this, the focus would have remained on the method or the strategy to attain these feelings and needs, not the feelings and needs themselves, which are the main core, main root cause of the driving force that inspired that strategy/method in the first place.


The difference here is that former focus points directly at the root causes that offer much better understanding of what is truly sought after, while the latter focus is limited to a surface view only, making it more difficult to accurately know what exactly is being desired here.


The unaware beginner was only concerned with the end result, the final product along with the plan to attain it. The problem, is that the unaware beginner is unaware why the final product is what it is, trusting that the partner is competent to know what they truly want without any need to verify if its true. The experienced specialist does not accept what the partner wants at face value, but reads through the lines to reveal the root causes where the plan even originated from and why. The specialist trusts the partners intention, but always does a double check just to be sure. The penalty for failing to verify what my partner actually, truly, really wants, can end up being excessively costly and damaging when tons of effort, time and resources are poured into developing something that isn't even what was truly wanted in the first place.


This is a very common mistake to make and it is very easy to fall into a false sense of security that a person is 100% sure of what they want. Unless they themselves are accutely aware and knowledgeable of NVC or any similar practices, there is a high enough risk that their plan/method/strategy is actually a flawed or even counterintuitive idea that will not fulfill what they desire.

They might even be at loss for words for what needs and feelings even exist, so I may need to help them reconnect with that repressed natural human functionality by whatever means I find most convinient. One such simple method is by using a printed list of feelings and list of needs, asking them what they're wanting to feel from the game they are making and ask them to point at the word that they think suits best their feeling.


This can take a while, but gradually a vocabulary of feelings and needs can be restored and memorized for any person, no matter how badly they have been repressed in the past. Going through this process is greatly valuable both in the short and long run. As I teach more clearer communication methods than what they have when no feelings and needs are involved, I will reap massive benefits in improved trust, understanding, clarity and openness, allowing me to connect and bond with my partners far easier and stronger.

Something seemed off.

As an example if I was making a succesor to an existing game, that would basically mean:


  • Improved graphics by making them more complicated and tedious to make.

  • Improved sounds and music to be of more expensive quality to produce.

  • Add more content, expand the length of story, double the amount of levels.

  • Hire more people, throw more money at the project, advertise the fuck out of it.

  • Add more details, more polish, more filler for any negative space in the game.

  • Upgrade gameplay mechanics with more advanced functionality. More complexity.

  • Add popular features that others have, such as leaderboards and multiplayer.

  • Add support to as many platforms, controllers and interfacing devices (VR) as possible.

  • Listen to modern trends and try to incorporate them within the new game.

  • Create more impressive visual effects with increased layered complexity and quality.

  • Come up with more innovative/creative designs for characters, objects and worlds.

  • Aim for the largest possible audience by making a foolproof tutorial.

  • Sanitize controversial/offensive/provoking content to appeal to bigger outlets.


So with all this, a better game is guaranteed? Hah. No. Absolutely not.


Back when I was an unaware beginner, all of these would have sounded like the best ideas ever. How could one ever go wrong with any of these upgrades? They're all popular, more advanced, more complex, more detailed than the predecessors. Wheres the problem?


The things I listed are things that are added just for the sake of it, because they sounded cool, were popular, were traditional (as in this is how its always been done), were successful or effective elsewhere or because someone asked, demanded or desired of it. Not because the purpose of the project demanded it.


Many of the things on the list might not even be compatible with the purpose of the game, often outright ruining it or causing huge damage, in the sense that it fails to satisfy what the creator was intending to achieve. Some of the items on the list might be good, but there is no reference, absolute certainty nor any guarantee that its even close to fulfilling the full potential of the project, let alone fulfilling its purpose in the direction its even supposed to.


This has been the case with countless different project I've seen or played. Each time this mistake keeps getting repeated, I feel sad because I really wanted the developer to succeed and have created a great game. So much lost effort and wasted potential has happened, often bankrupting the team and possibly even causing them to burn out, unable to make more. Nobody wins. Everyone looses.


I learned all this the hard way; by experiencing it myself with my own work and also through playing the games that others had made. It was these failures that gave me learning experience to understanding what was going wrong, with each failure and dissapointment mounting up consistently, repeating as a pattern until I could notice it.


The reason why the common mistake happens to myself and countless other developers comes to me as no surprise. While it sure is a flawed way of thinking to approach a project, its complete understandable where it comes from and why it happens.


Its because the most convenient, most available, first, easiest, low-cost action a human being can take is to look at the problem at face value, without understanding the underlying root causes of why something is really a problem. Its the most natural consequence anyone would do if they do not have the information that would otherwise allow them to be aware of the failures of this approach to avoid them before they could happen.


I want to clarify the phrases "most convinient" and "easiest" here, as I believe they might be misinterpreted to mean "humans are unwilling to do anything complicated". That's not what I mean. I want to highlight that many variables go into any decisions a human makes, sometimes making it look that they do something really complicated and hard, but in reality that happened to be the most easiest, convinient thing for them to do at that time.

Question Everything


The biggest issue with the common mistake is that any decisions, plans or effort is done without questioning it thoroughly. The act of not asking "why" as many times as needed.


Usually the belief is that if something is popular, that it worked elsewhere, that it made a lot of money, that its a tradition or someone said its going to be good, then surely it must be great by default. Why question it? It is told to be good after all, right? Not so.


The danger in this belief is that by assuming the good intentions of a tradition, forecasting statistics, trends, popular things or someone's word, is that I'm not making the effort with my own mind to verify if its actually true or not, or is it compatible or not. Maybe something might work in one project, but it may be the worst possible idea in my project. Maybe something has only been mistakenly labeled as "good" only because of the unrelated real good things around it, that masked the percieved "good" thing's actual true bad or useless nature.


I need to use my own mind and personally investigate on a case-by-case basis if something is truly good and compatible for my project, no matter how traditional, popular or good something is claimed to be. I must verify. Always verify. Never believe anything or anyone blindly until I'm sure of it myself. Doing otherwise is a design suicide.


There are many forms of traps that can lead to a common mistake:

  • Popularity. A large amount of people seem to like it.

  • Profitability. A remarkable amount of money/revenue was generated.

  • Trends. A certain theme, structure or system that has been repeated or copied often.

  • Tradition. Anything that has been around for a long time.

  • Originality. Anything that remarkably differs from anything else.

  • Someone said so. Be it a person with authority or a person you like, either one voiced their opinion on how they think life would be more wonderful for them.

  • Forecasting & Statistics. Based on information gathered from past, there is an attempt to predict the future while looking backwards, hoping to arrive at said future. A lot of expectations, no guarantees.

  • Mistaking one for all. A belief is someone failed at something, it means that whatever they were doing is forever impossible for anyone else too, even if in reality the original person just approached a problem with the wrong tools or was otherwise incompetent for the task.


These and many more are called Cognitive Biases.


In the event of a lack of information, time, willingness or other resources necessary to properly process new information, a human may resort to a mental shortcut to judge something in order to resolve it, often compromising rationality, reason and sense in the process. This can easily lead to stupidity, causing the designer to make decisions that may seriously harm the project, often without awareness of the caused damage.


The list of cognitive biases keeps growing with each year and there are over a hundred of them so far. There are plenty of resources and other material that cover cognitive biases in great detail, both on the internet and in books, but I find them largely irrelevant to cover them all as they all originate from the same source.


Its enough to become aware of only a few cognitive biases to understand the logic behing them, making it trivial to defeat them all afterwards, including the ones that haven't even been defined yet. Once I'm aware what a cognitive bias is as a concept, I can notice instantly if I'm about to fall into a the cognitive bias trap, preventing the mistake before it can happen.


The crux of a cognitive bias is to simply believe something is true without verifying it for yourself. To blindly trust that something is good without questioning why.


Once I know this simple concept, I can easily stop myself as soon as I notice that I'm about to make a design decision without being fully sure of its consequences and effects. I know for a fact that a desicions made out of a cognitive bias can destroy my entire project, so I rapidly get the feeling of concern and have my strong need for success triggered as a result, providing an instantaneous realization that I need to stop, step back and verify the information for myself in whatever way I can, until I am fully confident what consequences my design decision is certain to contain. And only then continue with implementation, never before.


The cost of neglecting verification is far worse than allowing myself to cut corners with cognitive biases. I'm talking about years of work going down the toilet. A huge portion of my human life will be wasted if I commit this mistake. Not only that, but I may potentially create a product that causes massive dissapointment or even ends up being destructive or harmful to its users or even the entire world by accident. Its just not worth it.


Even worse, I may end up stuck in an infinite cycle of frustration, pain and confusion, lasting for years on without an end, never knowing why any of my genuine attempts at improving or polishing a project only seem to make it worse, leave me forever unsatisfied.


Finding out years later that my efforts and creations were the worst possible design I could have done can feel very painful, especially if a previous project was already released and gotten popular in whatever state it was in.


Make no mistake, video games can have a global effect on humanity. Your creations can become popular and concretely change the world with their existence. All major worldwide changes in society, culture and industry always stem from the mindset of the people. Games, among other forms of art and media, can alter this mindset with new information, encouragement, suggestions and proposals, regardless if they were intended or not. Games can speak a powerful message through their very design that can have serious, long-reaching, real-life consequences.


For this reason, it is critically important to understand the enourmous amount of responsibility I have as a game designer and do my best to question everything I do thoroughly before commiting to any design decision.


As such, it is extremely helpful to know when to stop forcing more "improvements" to a project blindly and actually ask the most important question sooner, rather then later. Its far more satisfying, painless and easier to become aware of the common mistake as early as possible, knowing that all and any of my future efforts will not get destroyed by lack of information nor make the world a worse place to live by accident.

The Holy Grail of Game Design:


The Holy Grail of Game Design or "the best way to make game design" is this:


  1. Understanding of Technology

  2. Understanding of Human Beings

  3. Clear Purpose of the Project

  4. +Take into account any Business Needs


In other words, that means:

  • Understand the development platforms and tools along with their capabilities/limitations.

  • Understand how a human being thinks, behaves and works. Know human nature.

  • Be fully aware of what the purpose of the project is. Why bother make it at all?


These three parts are concerned with game design in effort to make the best possible game. The fourth part of the holy grail is unfortunately a necessary management aspect that controls the scope of the project. It pulls in the opposite direction of the best possible game experience, but since without it the game cannot become a reality, it must be taken into account as another critical need that must be satisfied besides the other three for a project to succeed. In other words, the fourth part means:


  • Take inventory of the available or acquirable time, resources, money, hardware, software, expertise, support that can be realistically and concretely alloted for the project without any doubt. Also how to ensure survival and sustainability, be it personal, team or company, to complete the project without death, bankruptcy or other management-related failure.



So a perfect game design project is where all needs of these four parts are satisfied. The platform and technology is kept within its operational limits, never overdrawing or bending it in ways it cannot support. The human user is respected morally, treated well and the interface/controls are designed in a way that is most convinient for a human being. The project itself has a clear purpose its aiming to do that is in line with the fundamental human needs, satisfying them without violating them. And finally, the logistical, management and business requirements for sustainable and succesful operation are to be taken into account to plan out how big and complex can the developer afford to make a project.


All four parts are critical and they're all interconnected and depend on one another. Success is only possible when all four of these needs are satisfied. If even one of these parts/pillars are neglected or fail, the entire project is at risk of total failure.

There is the Technical Side to this and the Mindset Side to this. Both are critical to the success of a project. The Mindset explains how to think, relate and decide during design decisions, while the Technical explains the step by step method to building a successful project as well as a collection of tools that are indispensable.

There will also be some further explanation why this works, extra topics, warnings and opportunities that are all greatly important in the grand scheme of things, so please pay attention to all of it.


Perfect game design is about meeting the needs of the Human side, Technology side, the Purpose and the Business side all in tandem, without compromising any of them in any way.



Origins of popularity, trends, etc.


An important part to recognize is that things like popularity, trends, tradition, profits, forecasting, statistics and such other things are only the final result, not the process or path to achieve this final result. To an unaware beginner, a certain feature in another project may appear as if it is responsible for the popularity of the game, but games are never composed of a single thing. Features cannot exist in a vacuum, since in a video game everything is interconnected. More over, features that end up becoming popular are originally developed alongside everything else in the game as a whole, so they all affect one another.


In the specific context of "hey, lets add multiplayer to our game because its a popular feature to have!", the feature that was popular in the game that originally made that particular feature popular is now taken out of context, out of its element where it was fully supported by all the other systems in the game in a dedicated way. To blindly expect that it will work without its original environment, out of context, possibly for a game structure that does not even support it is to risk months or even years of work for possible failure and not even know why it failed.


Game design that often ends up being popular by its nature requires all of the elements of the game to be designed in tandem, with each part supporting one another to fulfill the common purpose.


More over, popularity is earned as a result of a design that serves to create value to the player as a primary focus . Popularity is only a side effect at the destination, not the road nor the destination itself. Same goes for originality, profitability, trends and all others.


I feel annoyed whenever some artists genuinely advise aspiring artists to be "unique", "original" or to "stand out of the crowd", as I'd like those people to understand that what they're advising is not how they themselves attained uniqueness, but that the originality was a side effect of the final result they were aiming for, not the process itself that created the path to achieve that side effect. Striving for innovation for the sake of innovation pulls in the opposite direction of just making something genuine for a real purpose, that it in itself ends up being innovate as a natural side effect.


Its counterproductive advice to tell people to be "original", as it only serves to encourage to pursue originality for the sake of originality. This only serves confuse and frustrate artists, designers, developers and so on. I do recognize that the intention may not to have this effect, but unfortunately this is what happens when this advice or any similar advice is given. Its destructive, not just useless.


Most often, unaware designers that add features into a game out of popularity or any of the other traps I mentioned, do so without fully understanding why did that feature make the old original game better. They were only concerned that those things must be included in the game, as if they will "automatically make it better". No one is asking why they were beneficial to the original game in the first place.


In some cases, going by this way of thinking might seemingly work out for a while, but when it eventually will not - and this is unavoidable - all you are left with is a feeling of confusion and frustration, that adding even more content, more polish, more complex systems, more impressive graphics and more other "popular features" into the game seemingly has no effect in making it any better. In some cases, it seems like they actually make the game become worse. The scary thing about this is that this realization may only hit you years down the line, when you finally start notice that something is off/wierd/wrong/contraditory about all the work you've done. This is something I've seen others as well as myself go through.


I'd like to be able to prevent that realization from taking so long or from happening at all with this book. Or worse, keep repeating the same common mistake and propagating false information to the masses that influences people to believe in concepts that are only partially true, but nontheless end up having a huge destructive impact on the whole industry.

Warning: Developing games for extrinsic reasons


While this is not part of the common mistake itself, it is one of the single biggest sources of encouragement that lead people to commit the common mistake. A facilitator for many to fall into this trap.


Up until now, all the text so far was intended for the well-intentioned game developer who's primary goal is to create a good game for the sake of a good game. For an intrinsic purpose.


There may be some of you that read this book with the intention to develop games for an extrinsic reason, that is, to create a game for the sake of money, to be heard, to be understood, to create a change in the world, to bring awareness, to teach, to research, to gain knowledge, to find meaning, to get love, to be accepted, the experiment, to play with creativity or for any other reason where the game itself is not the final goal, but rather using the game as a tool to achieve that extrinsic, outside thing, whatever it may be.


If any of these extrinsic things are strictly a secondary or tertiary goal for you, then you have nothing to worry and may continue past this section.


However, if any of these things are a PRIMARY goal for you, the main focus, the main reason to begin and continue game development and design, then there is a massive problem.


An intrisically good game and an extrinsic goal pull in the opposite directions!

The irony of that is that a good game is needed to achieve the extrinsic goal, so the entire strategy to develop a good game as a SECONDARY goal is outright suicidal.


Psychologically, a human being has an innate limit to focus on only one thing at a time in the moment. There is also a level of hightened anxiety generated by the desire to achieve the extrinsic goal; This directly harms any attempt at making an intrinsically good game. You cannot think of what makes a good game while you're preoccupied with thinking how to get something unrelated. It is not possible.


One only needs to look at the mainstream game's industry, only to find that all games made by commercial studios, corporations and companies concerned with making revenue, a living or a profit PRIMARILY, tend to be a clone, copy, rehash or a similar derivative of an old product, often with only minor, cognitive bias driven alterations.


More often than not, any popular or monetarily successful games developed and published by profit driven companies had once taken an actually remarkable game that was originally developed by someone else for the sake of the game itself, intrinsically, and then used it as a copy-paste design template for their own product, merely using their superior resources to give it more impressive audio-visual fidelity and larger marketing. Just trace back the history of most big companies to see it for yourself. There is always an originally intrinsicly intentioned game that was copied to make a commercial success. I guarantee this.


Similarly the entire edutainment industry has suffered tremendous trouble and failure in attempting to attain success in creating "games for education". Ventures in gamification have shown only short term retention too. This is unsurprising, especially if one understood that gamification is an attempt to manipulate behaviour, which happens to violate fundamental human needs when the user realizes this manipulating, resulting in only short-term success, but at a terrible cost and significantly decreased chances of repeating this success. No wonder the edutainment and gamification fields have been massive failures.


Meanwhile, any creators will often find themselves feeling paralysed for coming up with ideas, prototypes and concepts while they are preoccupied with the thought of a primary extrinsic goal, whatever it may be for them, besides money or education.


All of these things are both sad and amusing to witness, like watching a child trying to fit a square peg into a round hole or looking at a dog attempt to chase its own tail, but I'd rather stop this than let it continue.


This very same problem happens in almost every aspect of human society in real life too, in families, schools, workplaces and organizations, in the form of Rewards and Punishments. A book by Alfie Kohn, "Punished by Rewards", details why extrinsic goals are absolute poison for creating an intrinsically good product. He speaks it in the context of education and families, but the same principles apply to game design quite perfectly. I recommend either reading his book or watching any lectures Alfie Kohn has done on youtube.


While watching or reading his material, I suggest that 'extrinsic goals' may be considered as "Rewards" in this context, while 'designers/developers' would be "students" and a 'successful good game' would be substituted with "learning". Witnessing this material for yourself can be frightening in how accurate and effective this information is.


Aside from Alfie Kohn's material, countless studies and research has been conducted on the effects of Rewards on Students, or in this case, Extrinsic Goals on Designers. This is not something I'm making up, its very real.


To briefly summarize the effects of an extrinsic goal, there are atleast these reasons:


  1. Extrinsic Goals are not a motivator.
    *Extrinsic goals do not motivate to create a good game. Extrinsic goals only motivate to attain the extrinsic goal itself.

  2. Extrinsic Goals ignore reasons.
    *In order to solve design problems, there is a requirement to be intrinsically interested in the project's game itself to understand the core reasons. Thinking about an extrinsic goal clouds this ability, suppresses it and steals away focus from it to think about the attainment of the extrinsic goal itself.

  3. Extrinsic Goals discourage risk-taking.
    *Primary focus on the extrinsic goal prioritizes the extrinsic goal itself, shaping design decisions and the development process to favor attaining the extrinsic goal itself. This comes at cost of shying away from experimentation, thinking from what is best for the game itself, preventing attempts from delving deeper into trying new things. All this in the favor of certainty, safety and speed to reach the extrinsic goal faster and surer. The results in a lack of creativity, lack of innovation, lack of variety and progress, causing stagnation, preventing growth, endangering long-term sustainability and tiring out users due to a lack of variety.

  4. Extrinsic Goals undermine interest.
    *Its mentally difficult to care about the game your doing (which is a requirement to be able to actually make it good), when the extrinsic goal is constantly center and first on display as the primary priority.

  5. Extrinsic Goals are addictive.
    *If extrinsic goals are a primary focus, thinking about them can easily feed a loop of perpetual frustration, where you feel miserable until you get the extrinsic goal, but you'll also feel miserable and empty after you've attained the extrinsic goal. The effect is you'll keep chasing after extrinsic goals like a bad drug, constantly wanting more, but never feeling properly fulfilled. Meanwhile, maintaining motivation for developing an actually good game, to create real value, becomes increasingly difficult, effectively sabotaging your long term success and survival eventually.

  6. Extrinsic Goals create pressure.
    *Since an extrinsic goal and creating an intrinsically good game pull in opposite directions, naturally there is a sense of uncertainty as you're essentially trying to make the final result be the path to that final result. This creates pressure and anxiety within, as you're engaged in activity that is literally impossible. While being unaware of this fact, there will be a nagging feeling that something is off and that there is no certainty that the project will be successful or not, unless you're also ignorant to your chances of success, blindly believing you cannot fail even if there is no real basis for this belief.


In short, if your development is PRIMARILY driven by an extrinsic goal, I predict your chances of success abysmally nonexistant. Basically, you are doomed to fail.


The obvious solution is to never do anything for extrinsic reasons. Any results created by the development of a product for extrinsic goals PRIMARILY will be dissapointing to both users and the creator, damage the user-creator relationship, corrupt customer goodwill/trust and harm the creator's reputation. Its too costly as it has significant implications in making life less wonderful for you and everyone around you. It is the kind of thinking that will lead to annihilation, misery and doom. Its not worth pursuit, especially in the long term.


However, I'm not saying that ignoring that desire for that extrinsic thing is the solution, no absolutely not. The solution is not to "simply let go" or anything like that. Behind the extrinsic goal, there is a precious need, a good reason, but attaining it cannot be done directly. You cannot achieve an extrinsic goal by directly going toward the extrinsic goal. The extrinsic goal is the result, not the destination/process to get to that result. As such, the extrinsic goal as the primary goal is a failed strategy to get that precious need satisfied.


The thing to do instead is to acknowledge that precious need that is behind the extrinsic goal, that its important. The second thing to acknowledge is that attaining it via a game project as the PRIMARY priority is impossible. Next, upon acknowledging these two points, abandon any attempt at trying to achieve the extrinsic goal the direct way. Do not stomp down, smother or forget the extrinsic goal, just realize that trying to get it while primarily thinking about it is a suicidal strategy that cannot work.


Next, distance yourself temporarily from the extrinsic goal completely, forget it, put it out of your mind and seek to create something fun for the sake of it. Get inspired, get excited and get interested in creating something for its own worth. Once you do, now you can bring the extrinsic goal back, but only, and ONLY as a SECONDARY priority. You're primary priority will be dead-set to create the best product for the sake of a best product intrinsically, however at the same time, you will look for opportunities within the framework of that product to achieve the extrinsic goal, but only in ways that DO NOT compromise the product, DO NOT compromise the PRIMARY priority of making the best product for the sake of the best product.


If developed in this way, with this mindset, you can achieve the extrinsic goal. In observation, it will seem like you'll achieve it as a side-effect. This is intended and the best way to attain the success of achieving the extrinsic goal.


Be warned, this is a very delicate thing to screw up. If at any moment you end up reverting your mindset to having the extrinsic goal as the primary priority during development, you will be at extremely great risk of causing massive damage to your project, further nullifying any chances of success for both the product being good, nor attaining the extrinsic goal.


I hope you'll be able to heed to this warning and avoid learning this important lesson the hard way through personal failure that will likely involve in wasteful loss of precious time, trust and resources. Please do your best to avoid this mistake and the losses that will incur from it. I know it can be difficult to act rationally during desperate and urgent situations, prompting to do this mistake anyway, but I've found that knowing and reminding oneself about the dangers of this mistake can be powerful enough to prevent it. Please keep this information in mind whenever you find yourself trying to rush. Its never worth it.



Warning: Intention & Action are two seperate things


There is one last major mistake that unaware beginner designers can fall victim to. It is the lack of understanding the difference between Intention vs Action. Dream vs Reality, Expectation vs What Actually Happens.


The designer creates something, strongly believing it will work a certain way, simply because their intention was so. They just assume things will work as planned because they want it to be that, without taking into account the reality, the real world around them.


The point is that Intention and Action are seperate, different things. They are not one and the same. I may want my designs to work a certain way, but that does not guarantee they will do so.


I'm going to list some examples. I'll use the following format to explain the examples:


Story: Short description of the entire situation that happened.


Action: What did the developer do.

Problem: What happened.

Intention: What was the intention.

Mistake: Failure explained.


Some examples will be about a more specific case, while others may have a more broader, general case. Either way, they all cases that have happened in real life, in real projects, with real dissapointments and real damages, despite the genuine intentions.


Scenario Index:


[Scenario 1] - Malicious/toxic player behaviour.

[Scenario 2] - Players cheating/using cheats.

[Scenario 3] - Making education fun.

[Scenario 4] - Joke or reference caused a backlash of anger.

[Scenario 5] - Monetization intentions gone wrong.

[Scenario 6] - Concerned about longevity of the game.

[Scenario 7] - Trailer vs Game (or Initial Vision vs Final Product).

[Scenario 8] - Sequel project fails to impress fans.

[Scenario 9] - User acquisition attempts keep failing.

[Scenario 10] - Adopting an experience from another media format.

[Scenario 11] - Player Retention is consistently too low!

[Scenario 12] - Customers getting angry over business decisions.


Scenarios:


[Scenario 1] - Malicious/toxic player behaviour.


Story: A developer has created a competitive player vs player multiplayer game, but notices some players are behaving in a way that can be described as hostile, toxic and malicious. They notice that this makes people leave the game and give the game a bad reputation, effectively destroying the developer's business and future. The developer just wants everyone to have fun and enjoy their game, while staying profitable.


Action: The developer created a competitive player vs player multiplayer game, that puts people in a contrived circumstance to fight each other. Victory and enjoyment requires the failure of the other person.


Problem: Players began behaving maliciously towards other players during the game. The game community became toxic and hostile. Players leave, game dies and profits drop.


Intention: Developers wanted the players to...

  1. Grow in skill by using each other as opponents.

  2. Provide a challenge that they felt AI could not offer.

  3. Treat each other with respect even when losing.

  4. Make the game more social, by having real humans participate.


Mistake: The core structure of the game has defined that success, progress and satisfaction is validated when you win. Winning requires everyone else except the winner to lose. Victory is artificially scarce and only available to one participant at a time. The result of this is that, players that mentally buy accept/agree with the structure of a competitive activity, become susceptible to developing new, creative ways to achieve victory, either consciously or unconsciously.


Losing feels lousy, while victory is advertised as great, so a human will intuitively become more crafty at finding methods to achieve it more effectively. This is natural human behaviour that the developer is either unaware of, is overlooking it or just underestimating it.


Unfortunately hostility, toxicity, mind games, malicious behaviour and many other damaging behaviours happen to be the most effective strategies to achieve victory in a scarce, competitive setting against other players. Also the most convenient, despite how obvious horrible these drawn out behaviours are.


In other words, the developer grossly underestimated both how smart and how stupid humans beings can be to achieve whatever goal is set before them (as long as they truly accept/agree to whatever idea they are presented, no matter how flawed or stupid it actually is). The developer also failed at creating core game design that truly reflects their intention, rather creating what they think will respect their intention, greatly underestimating the depth of consequences their design actually has. This, in turn, comes from a lack of understanding about human nature.



[Scenario 2] - Players cheating/using cheats.


Story: A developer has noticed players are cheating in their game. The developer has a certain idea of what fun looks like and wants players to have this intended experience. In the developer's mind, cheating ruins the experience they crafted for the player. If the game happens to be a multiplayer game, especially a competitive one, it also ruins the game experience for other players too, think the developer.


Action: The developer used any or all of the following constructs in their game design:

  1. Artificial limitation of game content for any arbitrary reason. Such as...

    *Segregating player capabilities into classes that may only allow access to certain content, while preventing access to rest of the content. Class-based character systems are a perfect example of this.
    *Unlocking system that requires invested effort to attain ingame content. Either for progression, longevity or gradual tutorial-like introduction purposes. All the same.
    *Denying access to content because its "not appropriate to have at certain stages of the game". Similar to unlocking, but in this case the content "does not exist" until its appropriate for it to be inserted into the game.
    *Tying access to content through a random number generator based system, where by the player may only access a piece of content if a random dice roll allows it.
    *Any type of shop system that requires either ingame currency or real currency to access content provided by the shop system. Similar to unlocking, but this system operates on an "artificial economy" defined by the developer, meaning the price of access to content can end up being arbitrarily inflated or deflated for artificial "balancing reasons" instead of a tangible exchange that would make sense in the ingame universe.
    *Having out-of-reach content or systems that *does* exist in the game's files, but is not made available to the player, such as admin tools, debug functions or test/developer/development content.
    *Any other content, functionality, feature or system that can be useful to the player ingame in any possible way, but is restricted for whatever arbitrary reason.

  2. The game has any design structures that irritate or violate fundamental human needs by their very nature. These particular irritating or violating design structures come in countless different styles and flavors. The best way to identify them is to look at the thinking behind them, the logic that created them, the source.

    Its not useful or effective to list any particular examples, as they all are context sensitive, so I would rather focus on the source, the logic where they come from. Such examples of the type of thinking that create irritating or violating design structures would be...

    *"The player must earn X! Getting anything for free is wrong!"
    *"This sucks, but its how things are done, so get used to it!"
    *"The player should feel exactly how I want them to feel!"
    *"The player must behave as I say, or else! No exceptions."
    *"The player should obey the rules of the game!"
    *"If player behaves how I don't want them to, I will punish to educate them!"
    *"If player obeys the rules, they will be rewarded for their loyalty"
    *"As the designer, the player must obey and play the game as I had dictated!"
    *"There is only one way to play the game. Everything else is wrong, inappropriate, to be frowned upon and is punishable!"
    *"It has worked like this for a long time, so don't disturb the way things are!"
    *"Player needs and feelings don't matter. My game overrides that."

  • Filler content.

    The developer adds new pointless, superfluous content with the sole intention to make the game appear bigger and longer than what it really is. By content, I mean anything from items, characters, writing, story, mechanics, levels, worlds and activities. This content is arbitrary padding, included and forced to exist alongside all the other content in the game, regardless if it serves the purpose of the game, regardless of its necessity or regardless if it adds any value/impact/richness to the experience. Its just there as unnecessary fat to prolong the overall game lenght.

    Filler content differs from regular content in that while it is as new and unique as regular content, filler content only exists for the sake of existing, to keep the player busy regardless of how it contributes to the game's purpose. Regular content only exists to fulfill the purpose of the game without arbitrarily trying to keep the player busy.

    Examples of filler content include:

    *arbitrary collectables (Collect these random 100 apples that are randomly scattered around the world for a vague reason at best and no reason at all at worst, with the original intention just to keep you busy. It is seen as something to do, regardless of it fulfills the purpose of the game or not, even if its executed in the most satisfying way possible.)

    *arbitrary challenges (Do this random task under 30 seconds, just because I said so. Racing events, fetch quests (Deliver item A to destination B), arbitrary skill trials (do this specific objective with these specific restrictions and handicaps, even if it makes no sense compared to the rest of the game), timed arbitrary puzzles, track down hidden item(s) or character(s), climb this tower, and many more are just a tiny amount of what arbitrary challenges can be).

    *arbitrary game events/story moments (For no other reason than to prolong the game, here is this random forced segment of story that I want you to experience. Often these are injected into the game as side stories, often which seem to do little to contribute to the game's purpose.)

    *arbitrary features ("Just to occupy your time, heres this additional game feature about collecting these random runes that have nothing to do with the purpose of the game, but just give you superficial upgrades, regardless of their overall role in enriching the purpose of the game.")

    *...and infinitely many more of such schemes that are either random, arbitrary or superfluous, serving primarily to prolong the game lenght, while occasionally trying to hide its intention with superficial benefits to the main game. Enriching the core game experience, the game's real purpose, is only a secondary concern with filler content.

    Effects of filler content often manifests as feelings of annoyance, frustration and contempt from the players. Human beings are smart and they can detect if something is filler content or not. They can feel it.

    And when they do feel it strongly enough, players will lose respect towards the developer, towards the game, immersion and enjoyment suffers significantly. It becomes difficult to treat the game as anything more than a toy at that point, even if it has potential to be something that surpasses the definition of a toy. Filler content ruins the game.

  • Artificial difficulty.
    The developer modifies the existing short game by any means possible to become obscenely difficult, with a primary intention to prolong the lenght of the game through increased player failure and increased failure consequences.

    By this I mean, make the player fail very easily to make them replay a part of the game (or replay the entire game from start) many times, thus increasing the time spent with the game. It can also mean that each failure will have harsher consequences, like losing progress or even reseting all player effort so that they have to start from nothing again, which again, extends how much time and intense focus the player has to invest to beat the game, thus making the game longer overall this way.

    Artificial difficulty is most applicable to games where player skill based gameplay occurs, such as combat, puzzles, platforming and such others that involve player reflexes, decision making, eye-hand coordination or any other trainable skill, that is put to the test during gameplay, often with some sort of failure condition involved. Artificial difficulty differs from meaningful regular difficulty, in that regular difficulty applies itself according to the purpose of the game, primarily, whereas artificial difficulty applies itself according to the arbitrary desire to lenghten the game's duration, primarily.

    I feel torn that I have to explain it this way, since "a games purpose" is a huge and important topic that will come up in the next "Mindset" section of this guide, while artificial difficulty itself is difficult to define in any other way, since it depends on the context that ultimately makes one difficulty acceptable/meaningful/good, while makes another difficulty be artificial/fake/bad. I could list some examples and while they may be truly artificial difficulty for that case, it may not be artificial difficulty in another case. It all depends on the purpose of the game, in what its trying to achieve and why that game exists. I will still list a couple, but just know that the context of them is strictly about the developer's intention to lenghten game instead of making it better.

    Artificial difficulty can be achieved in many infinite ways. For each individual game out there, there can be countless creative ways to add artificial difficulty, as it is all context sensitive. Some methods include:

    *Removal of checkpoints or saving systems. Alternatively, make them more scarce than abundant.
    *Making the control system more complicated and less intuitive to make even the basic actions difficult to perform.
    *Making character movement and operation require more precision, to a punishing degree. Tolerance for player error is minimal or none at all. Very easy to fail.
    *Making opponents in combat have more power, accuracy and defence than what makes sense, making combat have little to no tolerance for any player failure, to a frustrating degree.
    *Removal or denial of essential interface elements, depriving the player of vital gameplay information. Player more likely to make uninformed decisions and make more mistakes, thus fail easier.
    *Making player's success depend on luck, using a random number generator or other similar random element. Player's own skill or knowledge is meaningless.
    *Disempowering the player's character in any arbitrary way to make them more likely to fail.
    *Empowering the opponents of the player in any arbitrary way to make them cause the player more likely to fail.
    *Trial and error structure of gameplay. Require the player to know certain information to not fail at a challenge in the game *before* they know of it.
    *Visually obstructing vital information with foreground elements or other intrusive visual effects that effectively blind the player's vision, even thought the player's ingame character *can* see and is *not* blinded. Most notable in sideview 2D platformers. The player is likely to fail more if they can't physically see what they're doing.
    *Forcing arbitrary restrictions and limits on the player character or how the player must complete a task in the game.
    *Make a certain challenge literally impossible to succeed at, but pretend that its possible, while urging the player to try harder.
    *Lie to the player, tricking them into failing at their tasks.

    Theres infinitely more than what I listed and they are often context sensitive, so these methods might even be real design solutions for games if their purpose demands them.

    That said, the effects of using artificial difficulty are usually feelings of frustration, anger and sadness in the players, making them more likely to hate their experience with the game, losing respect for the developer, stopping playing the game completely, and telling everyone they know that the game is horrible, making it less likely for friends to introduce their game to their friends. Artificial difficulty ruins the game.

  • Arbitrary unlocking/progression systems.
    Instead of making all game content available from the start, the developer instead makes portions of the game content arbitrarily unavailable by default, requiring the player to complete certain arbitrary tasks, spend an arbitrary amount of time playing the game or make the player put effort toward some form of arbitrary system that collectively measures how long the player has played the game. As milestones in this "system" are achieved, new content is "unlocked" and made available to the player. This way, the developer can stretch out existing game content over a long time period, making the player spend far more time than they normally would, had everything been available from the start.

    Possible content that can be locked behind this type of arbitrary progression can be game modes, difficulty levels, worlds, characters, features, items, objects, game system utilities, collectibles, artwork, customization options, story sections of the game, pieces of writing, entire gameplay mechanics and more.

    Problem is, the game is essentially wasting the users time, making them have to deal with arbitrary obstacles, contrived tasks and pointless restrictions, simply in the name of lenghtening the experience. The player's real life time is not respected and the player themselves are not respected by these solutions. The other side effect is that these unlocking/progression systems can steal the attention of the game, becoming the main source of meaning for the player, which once exhausted and everything is unlocked, leaves the player feeling empty to see any reason to continue to the play the game for its own merit. Furthermore, it can hurt replayability of the game by making old players that are returning to play the game again after a pause to have to waste time and effort to unlock everything once again before they can enjoy the game at its fullest.

    All of this combined makes the game worse in all aspects and can even have the ironic effect of making people spend less time with it, because they realize the manipulative intent behind these systems, they also notice what inane nonsense they have to deal with, when they came to play the game for entirely different experience instead of what they got. As such, not only players will be more likely to leave the game, but also have a feeling of sadness that their experience with the game was horrible too. Unlocking/progression systems can ruin a game.

  • Blatant recycling/repeating.

This repeated content does not enrich the game, does not make it better, nor does it feel valuable to experience by the player. It feels like unnecessary padding that would be better if it didn't exist. It devalues the rest of the legitimate game content and significantly harms the game as a whole, just as filler content does.

    Please note that this recycling/repeating is not to be confused with the context of producing more assets at less cost. That is actually a really good idea and highly recommended for any size of game projects. Remember, the context all of the examples in this section are about the intent to prolong the game's lenght or size, out of desperation and fear.

    Ways to recycle/repeat are as many as there are opportunities and different games; basically infinite. Most commonly, recycling/repeating is done with items, characters and sections of a world/map/level/space. Any of these assets are copied and duplicated under a slightly different name, appearance, layout or values. While recycled/repeated content is new and refurbished, they do not provide remarkable variety that would make them as valuable as purpose-driven, regular content does.

    Playing through recycled/repeated content does not feel satisfying to me, as my need for growth and variety is not met when I experience it. It feels more like busywork, a task that keeps me occupied/busy, but provides little to no value itself. Recycled/repeated content in the context of bulking up the game does not feel fulfilling. It is difficult to explain it universally as it depends for each situation individually. I'd like to cite an example, but it would require you to play a game that does this to feel it for yourself to understand it.

    For an example, I'd nominate the PC game Borderlands 2 for its gun system, which features the same 6 archetypes of guns (pistol, smg, shotgun, sniper, rocketlauncher, assaultrifle) that are randomized to have slightly different color, damage, reload and recoil values. The developer says that the game features millions of different weapons, but the reality is that they only have 6, while the rest are just recycled reskins that only serve to provide more busywork for the player to compare their values in hopes to switch for a marginally better weapon.

There are a numerous amount of these dungeons, usually having the same fifty pots that always contain 1 coin each, the same traps with the same triggers, the same reward chest at the end, with the same random angry mob of monsters. Even if all these elements inside the dungeon are in differing amounts and in differing locations and with small differing unique features, it does not feel like I'm experiencing any variety or growth. Once I saw one dungeon, I'd seen them all.

    The effects of recycling/repeating manifest as feelings of stagnation within the player, making them more likely to have their sense of immersion for the game be shattered, which often results in quitting the game all together. Futhermore, the basic moment-to-moment gameplay ends up becoming more laborious than fulfilling, just keeping the player busy than offering enrichment for their lives. Human beings are smart enough to eventually notice that certain content seems recycled/repeated, which can lead to loss of respect towards the developer, for them to try artificially extend the game in such a way that affects badly on the rest of the game experience.

    Again, please do not to confuse this type of recycling/repetition with the type that is not used out of the intention of prolonging the game out of fear, but instead to enrich its offerings or make the creation of the core game easier/cheaper/require less effort.

  • Tacked-on features
    The developer tries to force an unplanned feature on top of the existing design in order to extend the amount of time players should spend playing the game. These tacked-on/appended features can be very different depending on the context of what style of game it is and what opportunities make most sense for it.

    Some developers may attempt to make the game have more side activities that are occupy time with arbitrary things to do in between the main core game. Particularly devious developers may even lock the main game content behind these side activities, forcing players to complete the side activities if the player wishes to complete the game.

    Other developers may try to make the short main game more complicated with extra mechanics that force a slower playing pace, due to the added extra interaction the player has to perform to continue the game effectively. For example, a hunger mechanic that requires the player to find and eat a food item every 5 minutes or they will otherwise die. The extra effort the player has to do in order to find, pickup, manage, eat and keep track of their hunger-meter forces the player to play slower overall.

    Another option is to add entirely new gameplay experiences along side the main game, such as new game modes, minigames, additional difficulty levels, a multiplayer mode for a singleplayer game, a singleplayer mode for a multiplayer game or an entirely seperate companion external mobile game that affects the outcome of the main game remotely.

    Once again, to avoid confusion, the thing that is common for all these tacked-on features, whatever they may be, is that they originate from the mindset of fear, with the intention to prolong the game's lenght. The inclusion of the tacked-on features bypasses the concern whether these appended features fulfill the purpose of the game, or even respect the purpose of the project. I hope that its obvious that the exact methods I just mentioned (side activities, game modes, extra mechanics) are not destructive by their very nature, but are destructive only when used in the context of arbitrary game-lenght prolonging.

    Whatever the method for the tacked-on feature, the effects of using this tactic to prolong the game's lenght are the same as filler content, padded content and recycled content. They can cause feelings of frustration, disconnection and meaninglessness. These feelings become stronger the longer the player attempts to enjoy the game. Eventually these feelings will become strong enough to cause the player to quit the game.

    If the player analyses why they stopped playing and realizes the true reasons, namely the developers fear-driven attempt at prolonging the game, then the player may also feel annoyance, anger and dissapointment, losing respect for the developer. This can manifest as negative behaviour aimed at limiting or destroying the developers chances at future successes by warning other people to stop them from buying games from this developer, to spread information to existing players to let them know how they are being treated right now (tricked and manipulated implicitly by the developer saying the game is so big and valuable, when in reality its just padded with useless garbage). The effects can eventually cripple the entire business and leave the developers in a bad place, surrounded by angry people.

  • Time Gating
    The developer prevents access to ingame content or game progression by forcing the player wait arbitrarily in real-life time.

    Alternatively, time gating can also refer to the trick where the developer only grants access to ingame content or game progression exclusively at a certain real-life time period, such as during a certain time of day in real-life, during a certain season or during an event, forcing players to come to the game at that specific time if the players wish to get access or progress in the game.

    I use the term "real-life" as a way to differentiate between the actual, real physical world that we live in, and the term "ingame" (inside the game) as a seperate world that exists with its own rules, the game's own abstract universe."Real-life" could be considered as "outside the game" too.

    The term "gating" in general refers to the idea of having a person be required to pass through a metaphorical "gate", an obstacle that acts like a funnel that only allows passage to what you want with no other option than to go through it. In this case, real-life time itself is being used as a gate in conjuction with a programmed progression system that uses real life time as its guideline to measure whether to allow or deny a player's access to game progression or ingame content.

    The idea is to tie real-life time as a way to force the player to either wait for something to become available or to require the player come play the game at a certain real-life time the game specificies in order to progress or gain access to something.

    The line between real-life and ingame world can blur considerably with this entire concept.   • Arbitrary content splitting
    Arbitrary content splitting is about taking scarce content of a single complete game and splitting it between two or more seperate almost-identical parallel games.

The expansions, dlc and addons may even contain additional recycled repetitive content, among the unique split content, in order to make the sold product look bigger and valuable than what it really is. A really cheap and underhanded trick.

    The most notorious example of this is the Pokemon series, a game that introduces over a hundred unique creatures that you must find and catch, by releasing two almost identical versions of the game each year.The difference between the two version is that one has 20 of these unique creatures that are exclusive to one version of the game, but not the other. The Pokemon developer's reasoning is that you are supposed to play Pokemon games with friends and use the game consoles in-built sharing feature to trade these version-specific unique creatures between the two versions, in order to get the full collection of these creatures.

    While there is some validity there, since the in-built sharing feature of the game console that Pokemon was built for is interesting and unique to that mode of play, I would still like to be more blunt and just call it Arbitrary Content Splitting, since this sharing feature can be structured in other ways that doesn't compromise the singleplayer experience, or require the use of two almost identical version of the game to exist. Nor all the annoyances that come from even having two parallel versions of the game, one of which if your friend happens to have the same version as you do, so neither if you will have access to the split content.

    The ways to achieve arbitrary content splitting are as infinite as there are types of games, since for each type of game, there are many different types of valuable unique content is possible to split between two or more parallel games.

Anything that is a unique, one-of-a-kind instance of something.

Or they may take a set of ingame campaign missions and release half of them in one game, while the other half in another, otherwise identical game.

All in order to make the player spend more time with the game and make it look bigger than it really is. I'm saying this because there are also legitimate business reasons to use non-arbitrary content splitting in order for the developer/company to get adequate compensation and support from their customers for the amount of work they do. Game development can be expensive and the players are the ones that make it possible with their support and contributions, so it does not make sense for the developer to stretch themselves thin by doing more work than they are getting support/money/compensation for. So just keep that in mind that Content Splitting in general is simply just yet another tool that can be used for both good and bad. This is where a difference in a video game and an interactive tool comes in.


Game: An activity/application which contains no intent for it to have real life consequences. Fantasy. For fun. For play. Freedom to fail.

Interactive Tool: An activity/application that is developed with the intent that has intended real life consequences. Serious application with real intent. Failure not desirable.


To further elaborate on what an interactive tool is: If compared side by side with a game, the way they work is similar, in the sense that both are interactive experiences and that they meet a need or many needs.

Meanwhile interactive tools do have an intention for real life consequences and are honest and upfront about their purpose. Interactive tools make it very clear that they are not games and that their use does have direct or indirect real life consequences or utility. Failing in a game is acceptable as far as real life is concerned, where as failure is not desirable in an interactive tool.

Translating Inspiration into Human Needs:


With that cleared up, now I'm ready to explain how inspiration can be translated into human needs and subsequently discover the purpose, which then will be attempted to be fulfilled with a project dedicated to it. The purpose then guides which method is best suited to fulfilling it, whether it be a game, an interactive tool or something entirely different.


Keep in mind this focuses on the game as a whole, not individual game mechanics within the game, I'll explain the process for those in the technical section.


The process of translating an inspiration into purpose is as follows:

1. First there is an event that happens in life. It can be a repeating occurance or a one-time thing. It can be remarkable or unremarkable. It can be something you heard, saw, touched, smelled, thought of, felt or otherwise experienced in real life. Whatever that event was, it stimulated an unmet fundamental human need in you. It surfaced it and made you aware of it.


2. This event brings out inspiration, also known as a natural willingness to satisfy the unmet fundamental human need. This inspiration then stimulated a request via fundamental human feelings for a way to deal with it, also known as a strategy to meet the unmet fundamental human need.

3. This strategy to meet a fundamental human need is also known as a vision, an idea. It is one of many infinite ways the inspiration can be manifested. Its a only a thought of one possible way to meet a need. It can be continuous satisfier that a user can return to repeateadly or a single-time satisfier that is effective only once in a lifetime per each individual person. Sometimes the vision/idea is vague to the point of being unknown, sometimes its very clear to the point of being obvious on how it would all work.


There is no guarantee if a strategy to meet the fundamental human need is going to be succesful before trying it, but it is still a possible clue that might actually do it. Experimentation will be necessary if no other ways to find out if a strategy is going to be a good one are available, such as existing examples, data or information. You are always limited to what you presently know and have.


4. At this point, the relevant fundamental human need requires identification. We need to know what the need is. Using the NVC list of needs or Manfred Max-Neef's matrix, you can pinpoint which need it is via the process of elimination. Be aware that it might even be multiple needs, not just one. Strategies can be synergistic and can fulfill multiple needs at once. In other words, games or interactive tools can satisfy multiple needs at once, although said needs must be interconnected, otherwise the project is not focused enough to have a clear purpose.


NVC offers a process called OFNR, which is Observing something relevant to a situation without judgement, expressing how it makes you Feel, followed by what Need its related to, finishing with a clear positive action Request that states a doable strategy to make life more wonderful.


In other words, you can say "___ event brought out an inspiration in me, which made me feel ___ because I'm needing ___, and I think that the idea of [your game idea] is good strategy that might fulfill that need" can be a helpful method to get closer to the right needs and giving hints if the game idea is actually a viable strategy to meet that need.


The two NVC lists for feelings and needs are useful to have as a vocabulary of feelings and needs if you are not aware of what feelings and needs exist. They're also useful to finding out if whatever need or feeling you had in mind was a misinterpreted feeling or need that actually has some other feeling or need at its root.


5. Once the need(s) have been identified, you have now found a purpose. The purpose is to satisfy those needs. Only that and exactly that. Meanwhile, the strategy/idea/vision is the method to fulfill those fundamental human needs with.


To put it simply: Unmet needs + Strategy to meet those needs = Purpose of a project


With that critical information revealed, it becomes easy to understand what exactly you're trying to accomplish with the project. Motivation is no issue as you can clearly see what is alive in you and how you could make life more wonderful in regards to the unmet need. It becomes trivial to see the bigger picture of where you are and how to get to your goal. Each moment will be easy to solve whenever you're feeling stuck and uncertain what to do next, as purpose will point out the next right action to take. Each design decision will be easier to evaluate, revealing clearly which option is the best one at any given time, for any situation. All of this thanks to purpose. Even if you are trying something complete new and unknown, purpose gives direction on the next best experiment to try out.


Thats why Purpose is the most important thing in game design.


With this mindset, it is not possible to become lost, demotivated or confused while developing a game or an interactive tool, provided that the purpose is transparently clear, the needs behind the purpose strong enough and the strategy has even a tiny amount of potential/reasonable logic to succeed in meeting those needs.


Purpose is a compass that guides you:


So at this stage, the purpose is discovered and fully understood. You now have a compass with which to always know what to do next, even in times of uncertainty, overwhelmedness or heavy adversity.


Its not easy, its not simple, but its certainly doable and the ability to reset the compass - towards the next best action to take to make the project a reality and succeed at its purpose and also how to arrive at the best design decision - is always present and available.

It is possible to discover during development that a certain strategy to meet a purpose is a bad one, especially as is the case with the earliest visions, ideas and concepts that come from an inspiration at the beginning. The method is great in that it allows you to see through the vision, through the idea to see what human needs were behind it all, giving way to much better understanding to come up with new ways that are better to fulfill the unmet/met human need behind the intial inspiration.

I also must point out that good game design takes time, experimentation, testing, iterating, pondering, taking the time to research and analyse existing solutions and examples. It is unlikely to come up with the best solution to each game design decision instantly. More often than not, sometimes you might only get a general feeling of a direction of what is needed after connecting with the human feelings and needs, but the actual best solution will require you to test different solutions out before you can conclude that "this is the right one!".

Two very important things to be aware of about innovation/creativity:


  1. You can never know what the final result looks like until you get there.

  2. You are always limited by what is available to you. Creativity is the remix of existing elements. Or in other words, creativity is about taking old things and arranging/combining/presenting them in a new way. It is not possible to come up with something new that has not previously existed in one form or another.

These points are important as they dispel any false information about "creativity" and "coming up with new things" that its supposed to be about "creating something completely new out of completely nowhere" (everything always comes from somewhere) and relieves anxiety during periods when there is a lot of chaos, confusion and uncertainty about what you're creating and how will it even look like in the end. This lets you know its all okay and part of the process when working on a project.


The purpose-based mindset in action:


Thankfully through the process of NVC, which is "what is alive in me right now(connect to human feelings and needs) and how can I make life more wonderful now(knowing what needs you're looking for, its easy to come up with infinite strategies to meet them)", finding out the right direction towards the best solution in a project is always available at any moment, even if it takes you through a phase of experimentation to get to it. Thats the amazing power of the purpose-based mindset in action.


More so, purpose is excellent in the inverse too. For detecting bad decisions, bad features, bad systems, bad mechanics, bad concepts and so on before they can ruin the project or waste your time. If you come up with a feature and then test it against the purpose by asking "does this idea fulfill the Purpose or does this idea bring closer to fulfilling the purpose?", you can quickly find out if that idea needs to be removed or changed if the answer to those questions was "no".


Now, to reiterate how the purpose based mindset works in action. This is it in its simplicity:


Basically, at every single design decision, no matter how big or small, anywhere from user interface, aesthetics, marketing, gameplay mechanics, sounds, music, releases, updates, maintenance, story, writing style, details to anything related and between, all you need to do is ask:

"Does this idea fulfill the Purpose?"

or

"Does this idea bring closer to fulfilling the Purpose?"


If the answer is "Yes", do it.
If the answer is "No", change it, remove it or don't do it.


Again. Any decision. Any topic. Any situation. At any level. At any time. Any importance.


Thats all it is at its core.


It may sound too simple, but there is a lot of thought behind this question that can reveal the best way to proceed in every task while working a project. Nonviolent Communication provides further explanation whats behind all this and how it works.
