Combat dynamics:
Point of game is to bump against with your ragdoll character against opponent character(s) to cause them enough damage to disemember their limbs without losing your own in the process. This happens by trying to collide your held weapon or your feet against the opponents body parts, preferably targetting their head or upper torso piece. Each collision causes both knockback to opponent, but also recoil force to self, causing combat to have reactive physics movements that players can benefit from or need to manage.

All pieces of the ragdoll besides upper torso and head are disposable, each piece has its own hit points that when breached will detach from the ragdoll with a briefly active particle emitter attached to the dismembered limb, spraying blood as it floats away from the rest of the body. If damaged further or overkilled by even more damage, it will burst into meatballs that have inherited momentum and splash blood too.


Inflicting or avoiding damage works like this:
As said before, each part of ragdoll can wear a piece of armor. Exposed body parts have a set of HP points, but those can only be damaged once the armor attached to that body part has been first removed. Each piece of armor has its own HP and will shatter once its HP goes below the value of 1.
Hitting opponents weapon with your own weapon deals no damage to the ragdoll character but reduces the hitpoints/durability of both weapons. Weapons tend be a magnitude sturdier than armor or exposed limbs. Weapons also degrade with impacts against armor and exposed bodyparts, including feet.
Hitting an opponent with feet anywhere except their feet or their weapon will cause damage to the opponent's armor or exposed bodyparts with no damage to self/own feet. however if you hit opponents feet with your own feet, then neither side damages anyone - it will show as 0 damage dealt, thought usual knockback and recoil effects still happen. If your feet collide with the opponent's weapon, then your feet take damage based on the weapon contact damage.
if any other body part collides with opponents body, then both parties take equal damage - assuming they're both exposed bodypart wearing no armor. Some armor may have enhanced thorns damage value that inflicts more damage to opponent than another piece or armor or exposed body, so wearing armor is beneficial not only from a defensive aspect but also from an offensive standpoint too.
Every weapon or projectile contact deals a preset amount of damage to damageable targets that is always the same, there are no momentum mechanics to enhance damage. Poking is a valid strategy just as much as swings are. Each weapon, armor, exposed body part collision between opponents always trigger a small force explosion of recoil and knockback to both parties pushing those specific colliding entities away from each other, a brief moment of slow motion and damage numbers for both parties that linger in the air.

Players essentially manage their body, armor and weapons to think of the best tactics or strategy to rely on to come out on top. Melee weapons both protect and defend you, armor or body parts can be used to tackle the opponent at some sacrificial cost, ranged weapons allow attacking from a distance but have limited ammo and may break sooner if used as a melee weapon. A wide variety of weapons, shields, hybrids, armor pieces offer different styles of fighting that the player will adapt to.

Combat gameplay is deliberately slow and methodical, carefully positioning and rotating your character to gradually remove the armor from the opponent or even disarm them, either literally by dismembering their hands or by damaging their weapon enough for it to break. Fastest way to defeat the opponent is to dismember the head, but its far from easy as the rest of the body, armor and weapons of the opponent may often end up getting in the way of the your attempts to do so.


Managing equipped items:
One handed items are designated primary and secondary, depending on the order they were picked up in.
If both hands are empty and picking up a two-handed item, then it will occupy both hand slots.
Two-handed weapons can still be picked up if only one hand is available. The two-handed weapon's hand offsets determine where the hand goes, whichever is available on the ragdoll. Drawback of having only one hand for a two-handed weapon is that it offers no stability and will flail around awkwardly.
If both hands are empty and picking up two one-handed items, then first one will be assigned as primary, while second will be assigned as secondary. When discarding/throwing items, secondary item is the first one to be ejected, then primary. 'First In Last Out' principle.
Items are picked up based on proximity - grab the closes from the upper torso center point of the player's ragdoll. If theres a bunch of items, player may need to grab, turn and throw the unwanted items until they get the one they desire, like sifting through a pile.

Healing:
Player Character can exclusively grow back lost limbs by devouring food items or left over limbs from corpses in the arena through cannibalism.
If you have hands and they're free, pick up the food item or corpse meat, activate to devour it, fill up bar to fulfill quota to qualify a random limb growth. Food items and pieces of corpses from enemies can be used as weapons, albeit they're fragile and not that strong.
If you don't have hands but you have your head, you can go into hungry berserker cannibal mode to chomp on edible things to devour them directly. Again, this only applies to food items and corpse limb pieces based on proximity to head. Benefit of head devouring is that its faster and skips the hassle of using hands, but has shorter range, requiring you to really press and nuzzle into the stuff you want to eat, whereas hand users can pick stuff up from farther away.
Note that already damaged limbs will not heal through food or cannibalism unless the food item specifically does this. High Metagame idea:
Inspired by my Doom modded playthroughs where I kinda roleplay as a new character that has a personal mission to do in a local area, that once/if completed, the character will then live happily ever after. Then I'd move on to the next mod to play as the new character that is included with that mod, along with a new map pack. Sometimes I'd have recurring characters in different locations because I play the mod again but with a different map pack. Death in these cases is mainly a time-rewind of "oh no no, that didn't happen, lets rewind and do it right this time".

Generally the time rewind as a mechanic feels like a 'god power', which I think cheapens the experience contextually, affecting the stakes and consequences, reducing the meaning of the effort as the player plays the game. It causes me to think: "Why does anything I do even matter if I can just disregard my failures during the gameplay like it didn't matter. If death or failures didn't matter, then I'm just doing a foretold prophecy where I will always succeed no matter what. That makes any of the events in the game feel like fake nonsense, like any of my efforts feel like they aren't my own and that the story has no possibility to take another turn. And no, walking away from the game doesn't count as a valid alternative choice."

Time rewind has its place in long form adventures that are primarily about experiencing a story being told - a world being explored and poked at, where gameplay is merely a secondary (albeit fun) activity that gives player the agency to explore such games as interactive galleries at their own pace and put some effort to discover hidden things that feel delightful to notice (like show-don't-tell moments in Fallout 3 where you walk into an area and you can guess/deduct what happened there and even realize why it happened without anyone telling you about it in advance. Fallout 3 and similar narrative/exploration driven games like it are totally fine with relying on time rewind, especially if they're ridiculously long where a full 100% session could last tens if not hundreds of hours. Expecting either permadeath or even softdeath would be far too harsh.)

Meanwhile, pure permadeath isn't a great solution either - far too punishing, causes gameplay to favor extreme caution lest you lose progress if you try to experiment or try to be reckless.

So, I propose softdeath; the character DOES die permanently, but where they fell, someone else takes the mantle to continue their efforts. This is perfect for this game where its about an endless series of small low metagame campaigns generated by the high metagame. Low metagames are gauntlets of 5-15 rooms where you try to make it in one piece from start to end. If you fail, you gotta start over from room 1. However, its a game about dismemberment, killing and death so just as the opponent stay super dead, the player character does too.

In particular this works with the Mafia Takedown where the player is a vigilante that tries to solo kill the entire criminal organization on their own because they're the only brave one to try it. In terms of low metagame, each campaign is about the personal story of a single vigilante taking up arms to defeat the thugs that are exclusive to that vigilante and the location the events take place. Its a self-contained story. Once you win, that vigilante retires, lives happily ever after. Both the specific location and that vigilante won't appear in the game anymore, case closed good work.
